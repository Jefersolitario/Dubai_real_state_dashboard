## 1. Does buying below a hedonic or AVM value produce excess returns, and do below-model prices mean-revert on resale?

### Takeaway
People who buy well below market do earn it back on resale, but every documented case is a sourcing advantage (experienced middlemen, agents, buyers of forced sales), measured after the fact; I found no study showing that a hedonic or machine-learning residual, used as an ex-ante purchase rule, picks purchases that outperform. Individual sale prices carry a large transaction-specific component that does not carry over to the next sale, so "flagged sales later resell at fair value" is what noise, genuine distress and mis-recorded prices all predict, and that backtest cannot tell them apart.

### Cited Findings

**Efficiency of the market and noise in individual sale prices**
- Repeat sales of 39,210 homes in Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas and San Francisco/Oakland, 1970-86: city-wide real price changes show inertia (a change in one year tends to be followed by a change in the same direction the next year, a quarter to a half as large), yet forecasting regressions for individual homes have R-squared of no more than .04, because "there is so much noise in individual housing prices relative to city-wide price index changes." Predictability is in the index, not in the individual house. [ABS] — [Case & Shiller (1989), American Economic Review 79(1):125-137; NBER Working Paper 2506](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2506)
- Los Angeles, San Diego and San Francisco resales 2001-2012, with remodeling permits to net out improvements: the cross-sectional standard deviation of the house-specific component of capital gains was about 13% (2006) to 17% (2012) in Los Angeles, 11% to 12% in San Diego, 13% to almost 18% in San Francisco. Its size "does not depend on the length of the investment horizon": idiosyncratic risk is up to 60% of capital-gain volatility for a home resold after one year and at most 20% after five years. The author's reading is that "house-specific risk is more likely to be determined by a single shock, whose size does not depend on the holding period ... one can think that the shock is realized at the time of sale", and that "a large fraction of idiosyncratic risk may be driven by heterogeneity in bargaining power and search costs across market participants at the time of sale." [FT, job-market-paper version] — [Giacoletti (2017 draft), "Idiosyncratic Risk in Housing Markets"](https://finance.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/files/media/attachments/MarcoGiacolettiJMP20170116082355.pdf)
- Published version of the same paper: the idiosyncratic component of individual house capital gains "is large and does not follow a random walk", and its magnitude is associated with proxies of information quality and market liquidity at the level of individual properties. [ABS] — [Giacoletti (2021), Review of Financial Studies 34(8):3695-3741](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:34:y:2021:i:8:p:3695-3741.)
- Same pattern in commercial property (NCREIF purchase and sale data): the variance of idiosyncratic log price appreciation is linear in the holding period with a positive intercept; slope about 0.01 per year, intercept 0.04, "corresponding to an additional 20% return standard deviation independent of the holding horizon". The paper notes the finding is "qualitatively similar to what Goetzmann (1993) and Case and Shiller (1987) report in the context of residential properties", who "model a property's observed price as equal to some 'true value' plus uncorrelated 'noise'". Commercial, not residential, evidence. [FT, 2015 working paper; published as Sagi (2021), Review of Financial Studies 34(8):3647-3694] — [Sagi (2015), "Asset-level risk and return in real estate investments"](https://haas.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/Asset-Level-Risk-and-Return-in-Real-Estate-Investments.pdf)
- US single-family sales: after a house fixed effect (which absorbs all time-invariant quality, observed or not), county-month effects and characteristics-by-time terms, the log standard deviation of the remaining sale-price residual averages about 17-18% of price across counties ("The mean of price dispersion is 18% of house prices"; 16.8% in the calibration sample). In the authors' calibrated search model the seller-urgency ("holding value") component has a standard deviation of 3.93% of price and the buyer-match component 4.65%; each explains roughly 5% of the variance and 86.86% is a residual that "could be driven by a number of factors: such as unobserved house-level heterogeneity or renovations, or realtor bargaining frictions." [FT] — [Jiang, Kotova & Zhang (March 2024), "Liquidity in Residential Real Estate Markets" (earlier title "Search Frictions and Idiosyncratic Price Dispersion in the US Housing Market")](https://anthonyleezhang.github.io/pdfs/sfipd.pdf)

**Who demonstrably buys below market, and by how much**
- Los Angeles area, all recorded transactions 1988-2009. "Flippers" are people or firms seen buying and reselling at least two properties, each held under two years, grouped by experience (Flipper 1 = 2-3 flips ... Flipper 4 = 11 or more). The design uses homes that sold four times (A, B, C, D, with the flipper buying at B and selling at C) so that any improvement the flipper made is identified by whether above-market value "persists through a subsequent sale of the same property." [FT] — [Bayer, Geissler, Mangum & Roberts, NBER Working Paper 16784 (2011, revised 2015); published in Review of Financial Studies 33(11):5212-5247 (2020)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16784/w16784.pdf)
- Controlling for unobserved investment, flippers as a group "purchase homes at a discount of about 5.8 percent (s.e. 0.3 percent)" to expected market price and sell at a 5.4% premium. In the cold market of 1992-1998 the discount was 12.9% and the premium 8.7%; in the hot market of 1999-2005 only 3.5% and 4.5%. [FT] — [Bayer et al., NBER WP 16784](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16784/w16784.pdf)
- Discounts rise steeply with experience: the most experienced group buys about 21% below expected market price over the whole period (18.1% with house characteristics interacted) and "well over 30 percent in the cold market period"; the least experienced group about 3.4%, falling "all the way to zero" in the tightest robustness check. For the most experienced, buying cheaply generates 63% of the return and market growth 18%; for the least experienced, 64% of the return is market growth. The estimates "do not account for flippers' transaction or holding costs, meaning actual profits are almost certainly smaller." [FT] — [Bayer et al., NBER WP 16784](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16784/w16784.pdf)
- The middlemen "purchase properties at prices well-below market value and re-sell them quickly at, or above, market prices. The steep discount that they receive by buying from (presumably) desperate or 'motivated' sellers accounts for the majority of their returns"; they "operate more intensely during periods when prices are stagnant or declining". Novice investors who entered in the boom "earned little more than the market rate of appreciation and demonstrated no ability to foresee market price movements." [FT] — [Bayer et al., NBER WP 16784](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16784/w16784.pdf)
- Las Vegas (Clark County tax records), 1994 to mid-2007, 328,843 transactions; a flip is two sales of the same property less than two years apart (about 32% of transaction pairs). In quarterly hedonic regressions the buy-side flip dummy is negative in every quarter and significant in 38 of 46; the sell-side dummy is positive and significant in 39 of 46. Median economic profit (discount plus premium, net of what an identical non-flip home gained) was about $5,000 until late 2003, peaked near $26,000 in 2004 Q3 and approached zero by 2007 Q2. The authors caution that repair costs are unobserved, so "any estimated returns necessarily state the maximum profit of a flip", and that part of the larger discounts may be that "some flippers may have purchased homes in poor condition". [FT, working-paper version; published in Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 39(3):248-263 (2009)] — [Depken, Hollans & Swidler (2009), "An Empirical Analysis of Residential Property Flipping"](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/others/research/research-calendar-attachments/seminars-2009/sem-swidler070709-pdf.pdf)
- Singapore: "Agents bought their own houses at prices that are 2.54% lower than comparable houses bought by other buyers." The discount comes from cherry-picking and from bargaining power (the larger part), and rests on agents' knowledge of available listings and of previous purchase prices; it is larger in less informative settings. [ABS] — [Agarwal, He, Sing & Song (2019), Journal of Financial Economics 134(3):715-735](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v134y2019i3p715-735.html)
- Information runs against the outside buyer: "sellers are usually better informed about neighborhood values than buyers"; a shift in the mix of sellers toward better-informed ones predicts subsequent "house-price declines" in that neighbourhood, and the effect is smaller for houses bought by better-informed buyers. [ABS] — [Kurlat & Stroebel (2015), Review of Financial Studies 28(8):2429-2461; NBER Working Paper 19875](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19875)

**Forced and urgent sales: how large the discount is and how much is quality**
- Massachusetts, all house transactions 1987 to March 2008, hedonic regressions with neighbourhood fixed effects. Sales close to a seller's death or bankruptcy are discounted "about 3-7%"; foreclosure sales about 28% in the working paper and 27% in the published abstract. [FT working paper, ABS journal] — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER Working Paper 14866 (2009)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866.pdf); [American Economic Review 101(5):2108-2131 (2011)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.5.2108)
- The same paper separates urgency from unobserved quality. Death-related discounts are larger for older sellers, unrelated to the timing of the sale, and "very small for condominiums", which the authors read as "poor maintenance of houses by older sellers", not urgency. Bankruptcy-related discounts "are consistent with a true liquidity effect". Foreclosure discounts are tied to vandalism and the cost of protecting vacant houses and are larger for cheaper houses in low-priced neighbourhoods; overall and foreclosure-related discounts are larger for condominiums and multi-family houses than for single-family houses. [FT] — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866.pdf)
- At zip-code level, "the prices of forced sales are mean-reverting, while the prices of unforced sales are close to a random walk", which the authors interpret as forced-sale prices reflecting "time-varying illiquidity in neighborhood housing markets". [FT] — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866.pdf)
- Opposite conclusion from a returns test on bank-owned (REO) homes: comparing buyers' holding-period returns with those on similar non-distressed homes, "the majority of REO purchasers do not earn economically significant excess returns" and "the implied market discount is less than typical transaction costs". The lower REO prices largely reflect differences in the properties. [ABS] — [Harding, Rosenblatt & Yao (2012), Journal of Urban Economics 71(2):204-218](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v71y2012i2p204-218.html)
- Auctions, using a repeat-sales design to control for unobserved quality: properties auctioned in Los Angeles in the 1980s boom "sold at an estimated discount of 0%-9%", while "sales in Dallas following the oil bust obtained discounts of 9%-21%"; the discount widens when the market is weak. [ABS] — [Mayer (1998), Real Estate Economics 26(1):41-66](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i1p41-66.html)
- Seller circumstances move price and time in opposite directions. Boston condominiums in the 1990s: owners facing a nominal loss "set higher asking prices of 25-35 percent of the difference" between expected selling price and purchase price, "attain higher selling prices of 3-18 percent of that difference", and "exhibit a much lower sale hazard than other sellers." [ABS] — [Genesove & Mayer (2001), Quarterly Journal of Economics 116(4):1233-1260; NBER Working Paper 8143](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8143)
- Sellers with a planned moving date sell faster, and "seller motivation affects sale price, but not the list-price markup" — motivation is not visible in the asking price. [ABS] — [Glower, Haurin & Hendershott (1998), Real Estate Economics 26(4):719-740](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i4p719-740.html)
- Related magnitudes as summarised by Campbell et al.: a seller with a 100% loan-to-value ratio "sells for 4% more but stays on the market 15% longer" than one at 80% (Genesove & Mayer 1997); realtors selling their own homes get about 4% more and wait about 10% longer (Levitt & Syverson 2008). [SEC] — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866.pdf)

**Papers that use model residuals to flag "underpriced" property**
- A regression model on public online listings for the Salamanca district of Madrid, built to flag properties "listed with a price substantially below the market price". Evaluation in the abstract is predictive performance on asking prices only; no check that flagged listings were bargains. [ABS] — [Baldominos, Blanco, Moreno, Iturrarte, Bernardez & Afonso (2018), Applied Sciences 8(11):2321; arXiv:1809.04933](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04933)
- A "Price Anomaly Score" built from model residuals on all Manhattan rental listings (September 2023), with tunable thresholds for over- and under-priced listings; the abstract reports no validation against outcomes. [ABS] — [Sultan, Rafter & Nguyen (2023), International Journal of Data Science 4(2):97-106; arXiv:2311.17287](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17287v1)
- The closest thing to an investment test: an AVM trained on "over 50,000 historical apartment transactions in Oslo, Norway, spanning 2016-2022", with a simulation of three bidding strategies. Screening out properties whose prediction is uncertain "outperforms human evaluation in identifying uncertain investment opportunities", giving higher simulated profit margins and purchase ratios, and the authors present this as reducing adverse selection. It is a simulation, and it uses the model's uncertainty, not the size of the residual, as the filter. [ABS] — [Pollestad & Oust (2025), Quantitative Finance 25(11):1771-1788](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:25:y:2025:i:11:p:1771-1788)

### Inferences
- The return evidence is about access, not detection. Bayer et al., Agarwal et al. and Mayer measure what buyers with a pipeline to motivated sellers, inside knowledge of listings, or an auction seat paid relative to market. None of them found those purchases with a statistical screen; the researcher labels the buyer first and measures the discount afterwards.
- Genuine discounts are the same size as the model's tail error. Typical documented discounts: 2.5% (agents), 3-7% (death, bankruptcy), 5.8% (average flipper), 0-9% and 9-21% (auctions, boom and bust), 18-21% (most experienced middlemen), 27-28% (foreclosure, part of it condition). Against that, the non-persistent, house-specific component of a sale price has a standard deviation of 11-18% in the US studies. A 15% threshold therefore catches only the top end of real discounts and sits well inside the range that noise alone produces.
- Resale reversion does not validate the flag. A price below model value followed by a resale at model value is predicted by (a) a real urgency discount, (b) a one-off transaction shock (Giacoletti, Sagi), (c) a recorded price that was never an open-market price, and (d) work done between the two sales. It is not predicted by persistent unobserved quality, so the backtest does rule that explanation out for the units that resold. It says nothing about whether an outside buyer could have had that price, and resold units are a selected sample (in Bayer et al. the units that resell quickly at market are the ones professionals bought cheaply).
- For apartments, the "poor maintenance" channel behind some discounts is weaker (Campbell et al. find death-related discounts very small for condominiums), while foreclosure-type discounts are larger for condominiums. For an apartment market, that points to seller circumstance and unit-level attributes (view, floor, fit-out, tenancy) as the two things a residual mixes together.
- Market state matters: discounts available to middlemen were about four times larger in the cold Los Angeles market than in the hot one, and auction discounts about twice as large in the bust. A screen calibrated in a rising market will understate both the frequency and the depth of real discounts in a falling one, and the reverse.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that takes a hedonic or machine-learning residual as an ex-ante buy rule and tests it against realised returns on purchases an outsider could have made. Pollestad & Oust (2025) is a simulation and was read in abstract only.
- No Dubai or UAE study of flipper or middleman purchase discounts, forced-sale discounts or resale reversal was found.
- Genesove & Mayer (2001) discuss how far a previous-sale hedonic residual is unobserved quality versus over- or under-payment; the working-paper PDF could not be read as text, so that treatment is not reported here.
- Bayer et al.: the published 2020 version is described in search results as extending the data to 2012 and to 96 other metropolitan areas; only the 2015 working-paper text was read, and all numbers above come from it.
- Harding, Rosenblatt & Yao (2012): only the abstract was read; the size of the estimated discount and of the transaction-cost benchmark are not reported there.
- Unverified (search snippet only): Demiroglu & James (2018, Management Science, "Indicators of Collateral Misreporting") reportedly show that pricing errors in AVMs can create the appearance of systematic appraisal overstatement when none exists. The publisher and repository pages returned errors.

## 2. Adverse selection and the winner's curse when acting on AVM signals: Zillow Offers and the iBuyer research

### Takeaway
Zillow closed its algorithmic home-buying arm in November 2021 after saying it could not forecast prices within the range it had modelled, and the academic work on iBuyers finds that algorithmic buyers only break even where homes are easy to value and quick to resell, because sellers know things the model does not. The same selection works in reverse for a bargain hunter: the properties that look cheapest to one's own model are disproportionately the ones the model over-values.

### Cited Findings

**Zillow Offers: primary documents**
- Press release, 2 November 2021. Chief executive Rich Barton: "We've determined the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated" and continuing to scale "would result in too much earnings and balance-sheet volatility." The wind-down "is expected to take several quarters and will include a reduction of Zillow's workforce by approximately 25%." Third-quarter results included "a write-down of inventory of approximately $304 million" caused by "purchasing homes in Q3 at higher prices than the company's current estimates of future selling prices", with a further $240 million to $265 million of losses expected in the fourth quarter. [FT] — [Zillow Group, Form 8-K Exhibit 99.1, 2 November 2021](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1617640/000161764021000085/q32021991.htm)
- Shareholder letter, same date: "We have been unable to accurately forecast future home prices at different times in both directions" ... "by much more than we modeled as possible", with unit economics "swinging approximately 1,200 basis points from Q2 to an expected -500 to -700 basis points in Q4 2021". Zillow bought 9,680 homes and sold 3,032 in the quarter, ending with 9,790 in inventory and 8,172 under contract; the write-down came from "unintentionally purchasing homes at higher prices than our current estimates of future selling prices". The board judged further scaling "too risky, too volatile to our earnings and operations". [FT] — [Zillow Group, Q3 2021 Shareholder Letter, Form 8-K Exhibit 99.3](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1617640/000161764021000085/exhibit993.htm)
- The same letter gives the selection statistic: "we have been able to convert only about 10% of the serious sellers who ask for a Zillow Offer". [FT] — [Zillow Group, Q3 2021 Shareholder Letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1617640/000161764021000085/exhibit993.htm)
- Contemporary reporting: a KeyBanc analysis of 650 Zillow-owned homes found most were worth less than Zillow had paid; Zillow expected to lose up to 7% on roughly 8,200 homes still under contract. [SEC] — [National Association of Realtors, Realtor Magazine, "Market 'Unpredictability' Forces Zillow Offers to Fold", 3 November 2021](https://www.nar.realtor/magazine/real-estate-news/technology/market-unpredictability-forces-zillow-offers-to-fold)
- One of the iBuyer paper's authors on the failure: Zillow entered late, expanded into non-"cookie-cutter" homes, and bid above its model's predictions to win volume; owners of homes with hidden flaws are the ones keenest to accept an algorithmic average price (the "lemons problem"); algorithms miss features buyers value such as architectural style, noise and neighbourhood upkeep. The article puts total write-downs at $569 million, about $30,000 per home in inventory. [FT] — [Kiger, "Flip Flop: Why Zillow's Algorithmic Home Buying Venture Imploded", Stanford GSB Insights, 9 December 2021](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/insights/flip-flop-why-zillows-algorithmic-home-buying-venture-imploded)

**Academic evidence on iBuyers**
- Transaction microdata, 2013-2018 (iBuyer share in Phoenix rose from about 1% in 2014 to about 6% by 2018). iBuyers "purchase properties at a 3.1 percentage point (pp) discount compared to similar listed homes" (about $9,000) and sell at about a 1.5% premium, a gross spread near 5% that had narrowed to about 3.4% in 2018; roughly half of homes are resold within three months. [FT] — [Buchak, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru, "Why is Intermediating Houses so Difficult? Evidence from iBuyers", NBER Working Paper 28252 (December 2020, revised June 2025)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- Evidence that iBuyers price from hard data: observable characteristics plus ZIP-quarter fixed effects "explain over 80% of the variation in prices for iBuyer transactions, versus 68% of the variation in other transactions", which "suggests they rely on information that a standard buyer would notice but which is difficult to algorithmicize. For example, a bad house odor or a poorly maintained neighbor's property". [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- They avoid what they cannot price: relative to a 0.60 percentage-point base rate of iBuyer purchases, "iBuyers are 0.52 pp less likely to purchase a house with a 10-pp greater predicted pricing error" and 0.15 pp less likely for a 10-pp lower probability of selling within 90 days; they "avoid transacting in houses with less than a 50% probability of selling within three months." "Younger, middle-sized, and multistory houses have smaller pricing errors in expectation." [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- When they do buy hard-to-price homes they earn less: "even among the houses that iBuyers chose to buy, their realized gross returns were greater for easier to price homes", which the authors read as adverse selection. In the calibrated model "over half of the homes acquired by iBuyers are of lower quality", and without their partial ability to tell quality apart their market share "would drop from around 5% to just above 1%." [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- The mechanism stated directly: "an owner who can sell their house very fast in the traditional market only sells their home to an iBuyer when algorithmic error overvalues their home." The authors add that pricing became harder in late 2021 and that, unlike Zillow, other iBuyers cut acquisitions; even with better pricing technology "the scope for dealer intermediation remains narrow", with strong incentives to avoid "older and less homogenous homes". [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- Digitised property records bring in investors who use algorithms without displacing those who use judgement; instead human investors shift toward houses that are "difficult to predict algorithmically". [ABS] — [Raymond (2025), "The Market Effects of Algorithms", arXiv:2508.09513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09513v1)
- State of play in 2026: the remaining large US iBuyer reported a fourth-quarter 2025 contribution margin of 1.0% (3.5% a year earlier), said its margin "bottomed out in September and has been improving every month since", and credited "more accurate pricing, faster inventory turns, and disciplined selection"; homes on the market over 120 days fell from 51% to 33% of inventory in a quarter. Company statements, not independently verified. [FT] — [Opendoor Technologies, Q4 2025 earnings release, Form 8-K Exhibit 99.1, 19 February 2026](https://www.boardroomalpha.com/sec/open-8-k-2026-02-19-0001801169-26-000009/q42025formxex991earningsre.htm)

**The general mechanism: choosing the best-looking item from noisy estimates**
- "If we take these value estimates at face value and select accordingly, we should expect the value of the chosen alternative to be less than its estimate, even if the value estimates are unbiased." With three alternatives of equal true value and independent unbiased estimates, the expected disappointment is 85% of the standard deviation of the estimates; with four it is 103%, with ten 154%. The remedy is to treat the estimates "like the noisy estimates that they are" and shrink them toward a prior ("disciplined skepticism"). [FT] — [Smith & Winkler (2006), "The Optimizer's Curse", Management Science 52(3):311-322](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- In a common-value auction the average bid falls below the item's value while the winning bid exceeds it; "the winner's curse cannot occur if all the bidders are rational", yet experimental and field evidence suggests it is widespread. [ABS] — [Thaler (1988), "Anomalies: The Winner's Curse", Journal of Economic Perspectives 2(1):191-202](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.2.1.191)

### Inferences
- The bargain hunter's selection problem is the mirror image of the iBuyer's. An iBuyer's offers are accepted where its model is too high; a buyer who chases the listings furthest below his own model's value is choosing the cases where his model is most likely too high, or where the seller and other buyers know something that is not in the data (the "house smells" problem). Buchak et al. show the cost of this in realised returns, not just in theory.
- Zillow's loss had two legs: a forecasting error over the holding period (the 1,200 basis-point swing) and selection at purchase (10% acceptance). A screen that compares a price with a contemporaneous model value avoids the first leg and keeps the second.
- The optimizer's curse gives the size of the correction. Ranking sales or listings by residual and reading the top of the list as "15% below value" overstates the expected discount by an amount that grows with the number of candidates and with the model's error in that segment. The stated remedy, shrinking each residual toward zero in proportion to how noisy the estimate is for that property, needs a per-property error estimate (section 3).
- What surviving algorithmic buyers do is restrict themselves to homogeneous, liquid stock where the model's error is small. The equivalent for a screen is to trust a low residual only in buildings and unit types with dense recent comparables, and to treat the same residual in thin or atypical stock as a prompt for inspection, not as a discount.
- Raymond's result suggests where human effort pays: the hard-to-model properties are where judgement, inspection and local knowledge still earn a return, and where a model's flag is least reliable.

### Gaps
- Unverified (search snippet only): the widely quoted figure that about two-thirds of the 650 homes in KeyBanc's sample were listed below Zillow's purchase price, with an average markdown of 4.5%. The primary news pages returned errors; the NAR article confirms only the sample size and the direction.
- The Buchak et al. paper's journal publication status was not confirmed; the NBER page lists only working-paper revisions (latest June 2025).
- Zillow's letter does not use the term adverse selection; that reading comes from Buchak et al. and the Stanford article.
- No evidence was found on instant-offer or algorithmic buyers in Dubai.

## 3. Uncertainty quantification: how providers and researchers attach confidence to an estimate, where errors concentrate, and what uneven error means for a fixed threshold

### Takeaway
Professional AVMs never issue a bare number: each estimate carries a forecast standard deviation, a confidence score or a value range, and the research standard is now a prediction interval whose coverage is checked on held-out sales, with conformal methods the only ones shown to hit their nominal coverage. Error is concentrated in atypical, thinly traded, older or larger properties and in anything where condition matters, so a single cut-off such as "15% below estimate" is a different statistical event in each segment and fills up with the hardest-to-value stock.

### Cited Findings

**What providers and standards attach to an estimate**
- Forecast standard deviation (FSD) "is a statistical measure that communicates the projected accuracy" of an AVM: "there is a 68.3% chance of the AVM being within a certain percent of the value"; an FSD of 0.1 on a $100,000 estimate means a 68.3% chance of the value lying between $90,000 and $110,000. [FT] — [Clear Capital, glossary entry "FSD (forecast standard deviation)"](https://www.clearcapital.com/resources/glossary-of-terms/fsd-forecast-standard-deviation/)
- One vendor's convention: the value range should contain the later arm's-length sale price "approximately 68% of the time (one standard deviation)"; the confidence score is one minus the FSD; "High Confidence" is a score above 85%, medium 70-85%, low below 70%. [FT] — [HouseCanary support article on confidence scores and forecast standard deviation](https://support.housecanary.com/en/articles/12315679-when-generating-an-automated-valuation-for-a-property-how-are-confidence-scores-and-forecast-standard-deviation-values-related)
- European AVM Alliance definitions: FSD is "the Standard Deviation of the Error distribution predicted for a set of AVM results with a given Confidence Level"; a Confidence Level is "a predictive measure expressing the estimated Accuracy of each AVM result ... directly translatable into a Forecast Standard Deviation", normally on the Alliance's 0-7 scale, and "the degree to which the Confidence Level actually correlates with the Accuracy of the AVM result ... is key". [FT] — [European AVM Alliance, Glossary of Terms & Definitions v23 (November 2021)](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/files/eaa/Downloads/EAA_Glossary_v23.pdf)
- To carry the Alliance's label an AVM must give "a predictive measure of the estimated accuracy of each individual valuation", and test reports must show, at least, hit rate, median error, average absolute error, "Percentage of Results within FSD" and "Percentage of Results within 10% and 20% of Benchmark Value", "segmented by all key dimensions, including at least: Property Type, Geographic Area, Price Range and Confidence Measure", always out of sample. [FT] — [European AVM Alliance, key requirements for the EAA AVM Label](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/en/eaa-avm-label-accreditation/key-requirements.html)
- The assessors' standard notes that confidence-score methods exist in vendor products but "parameters and methodologies for these techniques have not been standardized and it may be difficult to compare similarly named measures produced by different vendor-provided models" (section 7.2); its glossary defines a confidence score as the degree to which several AVMs agree on a property. [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Automated Valuation Models (AVMs), July 2018](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Automated_Valuation_Models.pdf)
- 2025 standardisation in the US mortgage industry: a common confidence score, a percentage from 0% to 100% produced by the AVM developer for each value, built on a 10% deviation band around market value (the industry's "PP10" test) and usable in place of FSD, because providers' own scales ("92", "B", "High") were not comparable and "there's a lot of complexity and calculations to the FSD, which hinders its widespread adoption." [FT] — [MISMO, "MISMO Launches Confidence Score Standard for AVMs", 5 September 2025](https://www.mismo.org/about-MISMO/news/2025/09/05/mismo-launches-confidence-score-standard-for-avms); [MISMO release of 29 May 2025](https://www.mismo.org/about-MISMO/news/2025/05/29/mismo-releases-avm-confidence-score-standard)
- An independent AVM testing firm told US regulators that FSD is hard for users to understand, recommended the share of estimates within 10% of the sale price ("P10") as a universal confidence score, and warned that "many AVM users are not currently doing adequate AVM validation". [FT] — [AVMetrics LLC, comment letter on Quality Control Standards for Automated Valuation Models, 21 August 2023](https://fhfa.gov/sites/default/files/rulemaking_comments/Attachments/16315/14_AVMetrics%20LLC.pdf)
- US interagency rule (OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, NCUA, CFPB, FHFA): institutions using AVMs for mortgage decisions must have controls that "ensure a high level of confidence in the estimates produced by AVMs", "protect against the manipulation of data", "seek to avoid conflicts of interest", "require random sample testing and reviews" and promote compliance with non-discrimination law. It sets no accuracy thresholds and "does not set specific requirements for how banks should structure" the controls; MISMO's release dates its effect from 1 October 2025. [FT] — [OCC Bulletin 2024-17, 17 July 2024](https://comptrollerofthecurrency.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2024/bulletin-2024-17.html)
- RICS: "AVMs work best with widely traded homogenous assets, and their performance degrades with increasingly heterogenous assets or assets that are thinly traded"; they "will typically be effective for a percentage of properties; so-called edge cases, outside of the set of properties where confidence in the AVM is considered acceptable, will require a less automated approach"; and "some assessment of condition will remain a key criterion for the valuation process". Among the due-diligence questions it lists: "What level of confidence for an output is available, and how is that defined and calculated?" [FT] — [RICS, "Automated valuation models (AVMs): implications for the profession and their clients", insight paper, April 2022](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/april_2022_automated_valuation_models_insight.pdf)

**Research on prediction intervals for house prices**
- AVM point estimates "represent the most likely value from a distribution of possible values"; estimating the uncertainty "remains highly unstandardised in both terminology and methods". Comparing intervals derived from the model with intervals derived from past errors on a large longitudinal sales dataset, and judging both by calibration and efficiency, "model-based approaches outperform error-based ones in all but cases with very highest confidence level requirements", with results depending on model class, geography and data filtering. [ABS] — [Krause, Martin & Fix (2020), Journal of Property Research 37(4):308-339](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jpropr/v37y2020i4p308-339.html)
- San Francisco Bay Area sales, gradient boosting (mean absolute percentage error 18%; 95% single-family homes). Plain quantile-regression intervals cover far less than they claim: nominal 70/80/90% gave empirical 56/66/78%. Conformalised quantile regression gave 70/80/90% exactly, at the price of wider intervals (median relative width 0.62 at 90%). Intervals widen with size and price; "larger and older properties, those in both low and high-income neighborhoods, as well as those on the market for less than one year are more challenging to evaluate." [FT, working paper; published in Journal of Property Research 42(1):1-19 (2025)] — [Bastos & Paquette (2024), "On the uncertainty of real estate price predictions", REM Working Paper 0314-2024](https://rem.rc.iseg.ulisboa.pt/wps/pdf/REM_WP_0314_2024.pdf)
- Oslo apartments only, 26,362 transactions from 2016-2017, gradient boosted trees: 71.0% of predictions within 10% of the sale price and 93.8% within 20%, yet the narrowest valid 90% conformal set still had a median width "around 32% of the sale price". Standard conformal prediction is "too large in certain geographical regions and too small in others"; weighting calibration scores by spatial proximity, or using conformalised quantile regression, evens out coverage across districts. The paper notes that "house prices display significant heteroscedasticity and spatial heterogeneity" and that even state-of-the-art AVMs "yield very different accuracy across different spatial regions and price levels". [FT] — [Hjort, Hermansen, Pensar & Williams (2023, revised 2025), "Uncertainty quantification in automated valuation models with spatially weighted conformal prediction", arXiv:2312.06531](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06531v2)
- Norway, 84,975 transactions across 286 municipalities: calibrating a separate quantile per municipality "suffers from high variance when the number of observations in each class is low"; pooling municipalities with similar error distributions, regardless of distance, lowers the coverage gap, especially where data are few. [ABS] — [Hjort, Williams & Pensar (2024), "Clustered Conformal Prediction for the Housing Market", Proceedings of Machine Learning Research 230:366-386](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v230/hjort24a.html)

**Where errors concentrate**
- Listed versus unlisted homes: Zillow's published nationwide median error is 1.77% for homes on the market and 7.20% for off-market homes; in Greater Boston the error is higher, which a Zillow scientist attributes "largely" to "the diversity of the housing stock". The article's example home sold 22% above its Zestimate. [SEC] — [Boston Globe, "How accurate are online home value estimates?", autumn 2026](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/23/real-estate/home-value-estimates-zillow-redfin/)
- Soft information: what algorithms leave out is "difficult to formalize or not likely captured in data" (odour, a neighbour's upkeep), and pricing error is predictably larger for older and less homogeneous homes. [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- Thin markets: appraisal deviations from contract price are "particularly pervasive in rural areas", where over 25% of properties are "appraised at more than five percent above contract price". [ABS] — [Bogin & Shui (2018), FHFA Staff Working Paper 18-03; Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 60:40-52 (2020)](https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp1803)
- How an assessment standard reads a large residual: "Properties with unusually large residuals, atypical characteristics, or extreme ratios of model estimates to sale prices, termed 'outliers,' should be reviewed. Outliers are cases where it is likely that the sale prices ... are not representative, the data are partially incorrect, or the property exhibits atypical features that cannot be adequately accounted for in the model" (section 3.4.8). [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Automated Valuation Models (AVMs), July 2018](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Automated_Valuation_Models.pdf)

### Inferences
- A fixed percentage threshold is a different test in each segment. If the model's error spread is twice as wide for one group as for another, "15% below" is twice as likely to occur by chance in the first group. The reader's own split, about 5% of ordinary sales against 7.7% of resales below the line by error alone, is this effect; the flagged list is tilted toward the stock the model understands least, which is also where Buchak et al. find selection is worst.
- The profession's answer is to scale the gap by the property's own uncertainty: flag a price only when it falls below the lower bound of an interval with verified coverage (for example the 5th percentile of a conformal interval calibrated by district or building cluster). That holds the chance-flag rate constant across segments. A two-level scaling by segment is the same idea at coarse resolution.
- Intervals taken straight from quantile regression on a boosted-tree model should be assumed too narrow until checked (78% actual at 90% nominal in the Bay Area study); the fix shown to work is conformal calibration on recent held-out sales.
- External benchmark for apartments: in Oslo, with 71% of estimates within 10%, a valid 90% interval was still about 32% of price wide, roughly plus or minus 16%. A 15% deviation sits at the edge of such an interval, which matches the reader's 90th-percentile error of 15.6%: the flag threshold is, almost by construction, the model's own 5th percentile.
- FSD-style reporting assumes roughly normal errors. With a median absolute error of 4.1%, a normal distribution would put the 90th percentile of absolute error near 10% (4.1 / 0.674 x 1.645) and only about 0.7% of sales more than 15% below the estimate. The reported 15.6% and about 5% mean the tails are several times fatter than normal, so a single standard deviation would badly understate the false-flag rate; empirical quantiles or conformal intervals are needed.
- The gap between Zillow's on-market and off-market error (1.8% against 7.2%) is a measure of how much the asking price knows that a sales-only model does not; see section 6.

## 4. Evaluation metrics for a deal-screening task with a low base rate

### Takeaway
For a screen whose targets are rare, the statistics and machine-learning literature says to report what share of flags are real (precision, precision in the top k, false discovery rate) against the base rate, and to check interval calibration and out-of-time outcomes; a central-accuracy figure such as median absolute percentage error is silent on all of these because it is designed to ignore the tails where the flags live. The valuation literature agrees that the right metric depends on the use: it offers tail and dispersion measures, per-confidence-band reporting and loss-based evaluation, but I found no real-estate study that scores a bargain flag by precision on realised outcomes.

### Cited Findings

**Statistics and machine-learning sources**
- The base-rate problem, worked example: a test that is 99% accurate for both sick and healthy people, applied where 1 in 10,000 is sick, gives a positive result that is right only about 1% of the time. For intrusion detection, "the false alarm rate is the limiting factor for the performance": even with a perfect detection rate the false alarm rate must be of the order of 1 in 100,000 for two-thirds of alarms to be real, and a low share of true alarms "would quickly 'teach'" the operator to ignore them. [FT] — [Axelsson (2000), "The Base-Rate Fallacy and the Difficulty of Intrusion Detection", ACM Transactions on Information and System Security 3(3):186-205](https://www.profsandhu.com/cs6393_s20/p186-axelsson.pdf)
- On imbalanced data "the visual interpretability of ROC plots ... can be deceptive"; precision-recall plots are more informative because "they evaluate the fraction of true positives among positive predictions". Example: the same classifier point with 500 true positives yields 160 false positives among 1,000 negatives (precision 75%) and 1,600 among 10,000 negatives (precision 25%) with an identical ROC curve. A random classifier's precision equals the share of positives in the data, so that share is the baseline to beat. [FT] — [Saito & Rehmsmeier (2015), PLoS ONE 10(3): e0118432](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)
- "When dealing with a highly skewed dataset, PR curves give a more accurate picture of an algorithm's performance"; an algorithm that optimises the area under the ROC curve is not guaranteed to optimise the area under the precision-recall curve. [ABS] — [Davis & Goadrich (2006), "The Relationship Between Precision-Recall and ROC Curves", ICML 2006 / University of Wisconsin Technical Report 1551](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/4288a2d3-d0a2-41cf-a8a1-ced5ab28bcbb)
- Precision at k counts the relevant items among the top k ranked results; it suits cases where only the top of the list will be examined, but it "fails to take into account the positions of the relevant documents among the top k", and when there are fewer than k relevant items "even a perfect system will have a score less than 1". Average precision summarises the whole precision-recall curve. [SEC; the page cites Manning, Raghavan & Schutze, Introduction to Information Retrieval (2009), chapter 8, whose own site was unavailable] — [Wikipedia, "Evaluation measures (information retrieval)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaluation_measures_(information_retrieval))
- The false discovery rate is "the expected proportion of falsely rejected hypotheses"; controlling it, instead of the chance of any single false positive, gives more power when many items are tested at once. [ABS] — [Benjamini & Hochberg (1995), Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B 57(1):289-300](https://academic.oup.com/jrsssb/article/57/1/289/7035855)
- Selecting the best-looking of many noisy estimates biases the selected estimate upward even when each estimate is unbiased; outcomes should be compared with shrunken, not raw, estimates. [FT] — [Smith & Winkler (2006), Management Science 52(3):311-322](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-smith.pdf)
- Searching over many candidate rules invalidates ordinary significance levels: after hundreds of tested return predictors "a newly discovered factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-ratio greater than 3.0", and "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false." [ABS] — [Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), Review of Financial Studies 29(1):5-68; NBER Working Paper 20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)
- "High performance is easily achievable after backtesting a relatively small number of alternative strategy configurations ... The higher the number of configurations tried, the greater is the probability that the backtest is overfit", and analysts "rarely report the number of configurations tried". [FT, abstract and front matter] — [Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado & Zhu (2014), "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism", Notices of the American Mathematical Society 61(5):458-471](https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf)
- Scoring prediction intervals: the interval score for a central (1 - a) interval [l, u] and outcome x is (u - l) + (2/a)(l - x) if x is below l, + (2/a)(x - u) if x is above u. "The forecaster is rewarded for narrow prediction intervals, and he or she incurs a penalty, the size of which depends on a, if the observation misses the interval"; it "addresses width as well as coverage". [FT] — [Gneiting & Raftery (2007), "Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation", Journal of the American Statistical Association 102:359-378](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)
- A worked example of honest reporting for a rare-event property screen: an independent firm checked a vendor's "likely to move" flags against closed sales and found 19.5% of flagged owners sold within six months; the article sets that against about 1% for cold calling and about 10% for portal leads, while the vendor's customers self-reported 36.4%. [FT] — [Inman, "Revaluate's 'Likely-To-Move' score is right 1 in 5 times", 26 March 2019](https://www.inman.com/2019/03/26/revaluates-likely-to-move-score-is-right-1-in-5-times/)

**Real-estate sources that evaluate AVMs by more than median error**
- 48 performance metrics sorted into seven groups, each of which "focuses on a particular aspect of the error distribution"; the choice of metric changes which of five AVMs ranks best, and the authors recommend a shortlist of seven. [ABS] — [Steurer, Hill & Pfeifer (2021), Journal of Property Research 38(2):99-129](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jpropr/v38y2021i2p99-129.html)
- From the working-paper version: "the most popular metrics in the AVM literature can generate misleading results"; "it will depend on the purpose of an AVM model" which properties a metric needs; median-based measures are robust to outliers, so the interquartile and 90-10 percentile ranges of the error ratio are proposed as separate dispersion diagnostics; and valuers of loans "may prefer valuations to err on the side of being too low rather than too high" (citing Shiller & Weiss 1999). [FT] — [Steurer & Hill (2020), "Metrics for Evaluating the Performance of Automated Valuation Models", Graz Economics Papers](https://unipub.uni-graz.at/obvugrveroeff/download/pdf/9606995?originalFilename=true)
- A decision-based evaluation for lenders: the "density estimation and profit simulation" method scores a valuation system by simulating the profit of a mortgage lender who uses it in underwriting; the paper also names skewness of the error distribution and the correlation of a system's errors with "errors of valuation systems used by competing mortgage lenders" as selection criteria. [ABS] — [Shiller & Weiss (1999), "Evaluating Real Estate Valuation Systems", Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 18(2):147-161](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrefec/v18y1999i2p147-61.html)
- European practice separates bias from dispersion, notes that widely used measures include "the percentages of results less than 10%, 15%, 20% etc above the Benchmark Value" (the over-valuation tail a lender cares about), and insists on test design: "Blind testing is critical to meaningful AVM Accuracy assessment"; forward testing runs the model first and scores it when a benchmark value later appears; "real-life testing" ensures "no possible data leakage or retrospective improvements". [FT] — [European AVM Alliance, Glossary v23](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/files/eaa/Downloads/EAA_Glossary_v23.pdf)
- Assessors' standard: test on sales not used in calibration, and "sales that occur after model calibration can also be used"; the median ratio and median percent error "are resistant to the influence of outliers". [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Automated Valuation Models (AVMs), July 2018, sections 7.3.6 and 7.4](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Automated_Valuation_Models.pdf)
- Evaluation by realised money outcomes exists in the iBuyer and investment papers: Buchak et al. regress realised annualised gross returns on predicted pricing error and liquidity, and Pollestad & Oust compare simulated profit margins and purchase ratios across selection strategies. [FT / ABS] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf); [Pollestad & Oust (2025), Quantitative Finance 25(11):1771-1788](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:25:y:2025:i:11:p:1771-1788)

### Inferences
- Median error and flag quality are nearly unrelated. The median describes the middle half of the error distribution and is prized precisely because it ignores outliers; the flag set is made of outliers. Two models with the same 4.1% median error can have very different shares of sales beyond minus 15%.
- Base-rate arithmetic with the reader's numbers (my calculation). Let p be the share of sales that are truly 15% or more below market, d the share of those the screen catches, and f = 5% the share of ordinary sales flagged by error. Precision = p d / (p d + (1 - p) f). With d = 0.5 (a sale that is truly exactly 15% below market lands beyond the threshold only about half the time, since error is as likely to push it back as forward): p = 1% gives 9% precision, p = 2% gives 17%, p = 5% gives 34%. With d = 0.8: 14%, 25%, 46%. For resales (f = 7.7%) every figure is lower, for example 12% at p = 2%, d = 0.5. Unless true deep discounts are more common than about one sale in twenty, most flags are model error.
- What to report instead, in order of importance: (1) precision of the flag and precision in the top k, against a stated base rate, with the lift over that base rate and the implied false discovery rate; (2) the false-flag rate by segment (district, building age, resale or first sale, unit type), which should be flat if thresholds are properly scaled; (3) coverage of the lower interval bound on held-out sales, with the interval score, by the same segments; (4) recall only as a secondary figure, since the cost of a missed deal is small next to the cost of a bad purchase.
- A positive label is needed, and the residual cannot define it. Candidates: a flagged sale independently confirmed as arm's-length and distressed (court or bank sale, verified seller circumstance); a professional valuation at the time; or a realised resale net of costs. The Inman example shows the form: flags checked against a later recorded event, with a naive baseline alongside.
- Backtests should use only scores the model produced before it saw the sale, and should include a placebo: sales the same distance above model value should, under pure noise, fall back on resale by as much as flagged sales rise. If the reversion is symmetric, it is noise; if flagged sales with an independent distress signal revert more than flagged sales without one, that difference is the usable signal.
- Count the trials. Thresholds, feature sets and signal definitions tuned on the same history inflate the apparent precision in the way Bailey et al. and Harvey et al. describe; a final check on a period never used for any choice is the remedy.
- An economic metric closes the loop: a discount has to exceed round-trip costs. In Dubai the registration fee alone is 4% of the contract value (section 5), before agency and financing costs; Harding et al. found the implied discount on bank-owned homes was smaller than typical transaction costs.

### Gaps
- No real-estate paper was found that reports precision, recall or precision at k for a bargain or distress flag validated against realised outcomes.
- How rating agencies and lenders penalise AVM tail error (haircuts by confidence band) was not researched.
- The primary textbook source for precision at k (Manning et al., Stanford site) returned errors on every attempt, so that definition is cited from a secondary page; Campos et al. (2016, Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery 30(4):891-927) on precision-at-n for outlier detection was located but its text was not opened.
- Steurer, Hill & Pfeifer's seven recommended metrics are not named in the abstract; the journal text was not opened.
- Unverified, as in section 1: Demiroglu & James (2018) on false positives created by AVM noise.

## 5. Sales validation: what assessment standards require before a sale is treated as market evidence

### Takeaway
Valuation standards define market value by the conditions of the sale (willing parties, arm's length, proper marketing, no compulsion), and the assessors' standard lists the transfers that fail those conditions, nearly all of which sit at the low end of the price range. A model that reads a registry of every transfer therefore finds its deepest "discounts" exactly where the standards say the price is not market evidence, so a low-residual screen is first of all a detector of invalid sales.

### Cited Findings
- Market value is "the estimated amount for which an asset or liability should exchange on the valuation date between a willing buyer and a willing seller in an arm's length transaction, after proper marketing and where the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently and without compulsion" (IVS 104, 30.1). A forced sale describes "circumstances where a seller is under compulsion to sell", where "a proper marketing period is not possible" and "buyers may not be able to undertake adequate due diligence" (IVS 104, 170.1). [FT] — [International Valuation Standards Council, Standards glossary](https://ivsc.org/standards-glossary/)
- Starting position of the assessors' standard: "all sales should be considered candidates for valid sales unless sufficient information can be documented to show otherwise"; "A verified sale is more reliable than an unverified sale"; sales "should be invalidated only when they fail to meet the requirements of an open-market, arm's-length transaction", and those that do fail "should be excluded for use in ratio studies or modeling". [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales, approved April 2020](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Effort scales with how unusual the price is: "the fewer the sales, the less common or more complex the property, and the more atypical the sale price, the greater the effort should be to confirm the particulars of the sale" (5.1). Screening by ratio is endorsed as a way to find sales to check: "It is a best practice to further verify sales with an atypical ratio", for example a ratio "less than 50 percent or greater than 150 percent", or one markedly different from neighbouring or similar properties; but "sales should never be excluded from a ratio study solely on the basis of the computed ratio" (5.3.3). [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- "Sales generally considered invalid" (5.4): sales involving government agencies; charitable, religious or educational institutions; financial institutions as buyer or seller; "sales between relatives or between corporate affiliates"; sales settling an estate; "forced sales resulting from a judicial order"; sales of doubtful title. [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Direction of the bias, in the standard's words: government as seller, "values typically fall on the low end of the value range"; lender-owned sales "typically are on the low side of the value range because the financial institution is highly motivated to sell", usable only where they exceed 20% of sales in a market area and never if the property was vandalised (5.4.4); related-party sales "are usually non-open-market transactions" (5.4.5); a quick estate sale "may not represent fair market value" (5.4.6); court-ordered forced sales "should never be considered for model calibration or ratio studies" (5.4.7); "Sales in which title is in doubt tend to be below market value" (5.4.8); short sales carry "the element of undue stimuli" (5.5.6). [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Partial interests, bulk and auctions: "Transfers that convey less than full interest are rarely usable" (4.2.1) and a partial-interest sale "should be excluded as a valid transaction" (5.5.1.2); "Acquisitions or divestments by large corporations, pension funds, or real estate investment trusts (REITs) that involve multiple parcels typically should not be considered" (5.5.2) and multi-parcel sales covering several economic units "generally should not be used" (5.6.2); "auction sales of real property tend to be at the lower end of the price spectrum", "all absolute auctions should be considered invalid", and others qualify only if well advertised, well attended and with a reserve (5.5.1.5). [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Other conditions the standard asks about: exposure ("Sales of properties that have been exposed to the open market too long, not long enough, or not at all may not represent market value", 4.2.5); signs of an uninformed buyer, such as no knowledge of the local market, no broker, no comparison with similar properties, "bought sight unseen" (4.2.4.3); and units in developments whose common facilities are not yet built, where prices "influenced by promises to add or complete common elements" should be excluded or adjusted (5.5.1.4). [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Order of operations: "Sales should be trimmed for outliers during the statistical phase, not during the verification phase", and reason codes should record why each sale was judged valid or invalid. [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Verification and Adjustment of Sales (2020)](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- The AVM standard carries the same requirement into modelling: "Sales and economic data should be open market transactions" (section 7 principles). The European AVM Alliance accepts only "reliable Surveyor Valuations or Sales Prices as valid Benchmark Values" for testing. [FT] — [IAAO, Standard on Automated Valuation Models (2018)](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Automated_Valuation_Models.pdf); [European AVM Alliance, key requirements](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/en/eaa-avm-label-accreditation/key-requirements.html)
- Dubai: a transfer to first-degree relatives (parents, spouse, children) can be registered as a gift for a Dubai Land Department fee of "0.125% of the value of the property", not less than Dh2,000, under Executive Council Resolution No. 30 of 2013, whereas a sale, including one to a first-degree relative, costs "4 per cent of the value of the sale contract". [FT] — [Khaleej Times, "Dubai: Do I need to pay a fee to transfer my property to family members?", 8 May 2022](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/legal/dubai-do-i-need-to-pay-a-fee-to-transfer-my-property-to-family-members)

### Inferences
- A land registry is the "transfer document" layer of the IAAO process without the questionnaire and follow-up interview that turn a transfer into a verified sale. Rules based on procedure type, same-day identical prices, extreme ratios to a project median and ownership share reproduce several IAAO categories, but others leave no trace in a registry: related parties transacting as an ordinary sale, sales with side payments or included contents, court and bank sales recorded under an ordinary sale procedure, and portfolio transfers priced as an allocation.
- The standards and the bargain hunter want opposite things from the same records. Assessors remove forced, bank, auction and estate sales so that the model describes the open market; the investor is looking for exactly those sales. The clean design is to exclude them from calibration, keep them tagged, and treat the tag (who sold, by what procedure), not the residual, as the evidence of a deal channel.
- The IAAO advice to use extreme ratios as a prompt for verification, never as proof, applies directly: a sale 15% or more below model value is a case to investigate, with the prior, set by the standard's own list, that something about the sale or the data is non-market.
- The clause on incomplete common property is relevant to off-plan and newly handed-over apartments, where the price may reflect promised amenities or payment terms and is not comparable with the price of a completed unit.
- The fee difference (0.125% against 4%) means most family transfers should appear as gifts and be easy to exclude; a "sale" between relatives at a low price remains possible and would look like a bargain.

### Gaps
- How the Dubai Land Department treats a declared sale price that is below its own valuation, and whether fees are charged on the higher figure, was not confirmed from a primary source; broker pages state that gifts require a Land Department valuation.
- Dubai's own valuation standards (the Emirates Book Valuation Standards) and the IAAO Standard on Ratio Studies (outlier trimming, sales chasing) were not opened.
- The glossary page shows the IVS definitions with their IVS 104 paragraph numbers; the wording in the edition effective 31 January 2025 was not separately checked.

## 6. Listing data versus transaction data

### Takeaway
Asking prices guide buyers and track market trends well, but they describe a different set of properties from those that sell and are poor evidence of any single property's value; standards refuse them as benchmarks. A model built on completed sales describes what has already been bought: it can value an opportunity once one is in view, but it cannot show which properties are on offer, at what price, or from which sellers.

### Cited Findings
- The asking price is neither a ceiling nor a commitment, yet "many housing transactions involve a sales price exactly equal to asking price"; it directs buyer search, more strongly for atypical houses and in weak markets. [ABS] — [Han & Strange (2016), "What is the role of the asking price for a house?", Journal of Urban Economics 93:115-130](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v93y2016icp115-130.html)
- Belfast: in down or normal markets the list price generally exceeds the sale price; in a strong market homes sell above list as sellers move toward an auction-like process, and list prices are slow to fall in a downturn. [ABS] — [Haurin, McGreal, Adair, Brown & Webb (2013), Journal of Housing Economics 22(1):1-10](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jhouse/v22y2013i1p1-10.html)
- Sellers are most likely to cut the asking price when the initial markup was high and the home is vacant; homes with large percentage cuts take longer to sell and sell for less. A price cut is evidence of initial overpricing as much as of urgency. [ABS] — [Knight (2002), Real Estate Economics 30(2):213-237](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v30y2002i2p213-237.html)
- "An increase in the list price increases expected time-on-the-market"; listings withdrawn before sale had higher and more variable list prices, so data on sold homes alone are censored. [ABS] — [Anglin, Rutherford & Springer (2003), Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 26(1):95-111](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:jrefec:v:26:y:2003:i:1:p:95-111)
- Sellers facing a loss ask more and wait; motivated sellers sell faster and for less without signalling it in the asking-price markup. [ABS] — [Genesove & Mayer (2001), NBER WP 8143](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8143); [Glower, Haurin & Hendershott (1998)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i4p719-740.html)
- Tokyo, four prices for the same market (first asking price, asking price when an offer is made, contract price reported by agents, registry price): there are "substantial differences between the distributions of the four prices", but after quality adjustment "only small differences remain". [ABS] — [Shimizu, Nishimura & Watanabe (2016), Regional Science and Urban Economics 59:37-53](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/regeco/v59y2016icp37-53.html)
- Ireland 2006-2012, population-level listings and mortgage drawdowns: "an index based on listed prices is a very accurate barometer of, and lead indicator for, true transaction prices", even in a crash. [ABS] — [Lyons (2019), Finance Research Letters 30:228-232](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v30y2019icp228-232.html)
- Berlin single-family houses 2007-2015 (12,524 sales and 68,070 listings after cleaning): asking prices average about 26-28% above sale prices, "mainly because the composition of characteristics differs between the two data sets". "Listings data are not very useful to predict market values of individual houses": the ask-based model is biased upward with a larger error variance and puts 30.4% of predictions within 10% against 36.8% for the sale-based model. Conclusion: "ask data are no replacement for sale data", though an ask-price index is useful for nowcasting. The authors' literature review cites matched ask-over-sale differences of about 4% and 2% in two US studies (as summarised; check before quoting). [FT] — [Kolbe, Schulz, Wersing & Werwatz (2021), "Real estate listings and their usefulness for hedonic regressions", Empirical Economics 61(6):3239-3269](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805573)
- What a listing adds to a valuation model: Zillow's median error is 1.77% for homes on the market and 7.20% for those that are not. [SEC] — [Boston Globe, autumn 2026](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/23/real-estate/home-value-estimates-zillow-redfin/)
- Standards treat asking prices as contaminated evidence: an asking price "may or may not be met by the Sale Price"; "Other data points, e.g. Asking Prices, are explicitly rejected" as benchmark values; and in competitive tests any case whose asking price "may already be available to some of the AVMs being tested must be excluded." [FT] — [European AVM Alliance, key requirements](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/en/eaa-avm-label-accreditation/key-requirements.html); [Glossary v23](https://www.europeanavmalliance.org/files/eaa/Downloads/EAA_Glossary_v23.pdf)
- Dubai, comparison of average listing and average transaction values by segment (not matched properties): "The citywide residential gap between listings and transactions reached its peak in May 2023, when properties were listed for 52.67% more than their final sales value (on average)"; the gap then narrowed. In Palm Jumeirah villas average sale prices exceeded listing prices "by as much as 87.9%" after the pandemic; in Jumeirah Village Circle listings have been consistently above transactions since April 2023. [FT] — [Global Capital Partners with Reidin data, "Dubai Residential: Mind the Gap - Evaluating the Difference Between Listings and Transactions" (2024)](https://reidin.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Dubai-Residential-Mind-the-Gap-Evaluating-the-Difference-Between-Listings-and-Transactions.pdf)
- Reporting on that study: in Jumeirah Village Circle the gap between asking and transacted values fell to about Dh200 per square foot from more than Dh550 a little over a year earlier; the narrowing is linked partly to Land Department rules limiting a property to three marketing agents and requiring listings to be removed once a deal closes, which removed "phantom" listings. [FT] — [Gulf News, "Dubai property listings now show 'realistic' asking prices as new RERA rules kick in", 17 May 2024](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/dubai-property-listings-now-show-realistic-asking-prices-as-new-rera-rules-kick-in-1.1715921923236)
- Time is part of the price: the ordinary listing process often takes 90 days or more, and sellers accepted about 3% less for an immediate sale to an iBuyer. [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)

### Inferences
- A registered sale flagged as cheap identifies the buyer who already has the property. By the time a transfer is recorded the opportunity is closed; the record can teach where and from whom discounts occur, but it is not a lead.
- Acting on a deal needs the offer set: live listings, price-cut histories, auction catalogues, lender and court sales. The sales-based model then answers "what is this worth?" for a property someone is offering now. That comparison (asking price against a sales-based value) is the one the literature supports; a value model built on asking prices is not (Kolbe et al.).
- Asking prices are informative about the individual property, which is why Zillow's error falls by three-quarters once a home is listed. The flip side: most asking prices sit close to the eventual sale price, so a listing far below a sales-based value is a rare event, and by the reasoning of section 2 it is more often explained by the property (or by a bait listing) than by a careless seller. Genuinely underpriced listings also sell fastest, so the time available to verify one is short.
- Seller motivation mostly does not show in the asking price (Glower et al.), while reluctance does (Genesove & Mayer). Observable proxies are therefore indirect: vacancy, repeated cuts after a high initial markup, time on market, a lender or court as seller. Each is also consistent with an overpriced or flawed property, so none is sufficient alone.
- In Dubai the unmatched listing-to-transaction gap has swung from listings 53% above transactions to transactions far above listings by segment, driven by which properties are listed versus which sell. Asking-price averages cannot stand in for registry comparables there, and any ask-to-value screen needs the two matched at unit or building level.

### Gaps
- No matched study of Dubai listings against their eventual registered prices was found (ask-to-sale ratio and time on market per unit); the Reidin comparison is of segment averages.
- Unverified (search snippets only): 2026 reports that the gap between asking and transacted prices in Dubai widened to about 7% from under 3% in 2024, and that several hundred listings cut prices repeatedly in spring 2026; the source pages returned errors.
- Zillow's own description of how list price, listing text and days on market enter its on-market estimate could not be opened (its pages returned errors); only the accuracy figures, via the Boston Globe, are confirmed.
- Kolbe et al.'s cited matched ask-over-sale figures (about 4% and 2%) are reported from the summary of their literature review and should be checked against the original studies before use.

## 7. Practice: how professional investors and proptech firms source below-market deals, and the evidence that it works

### Takeaway
Professionals find the seller first and value the property second: repeat middlemen, agents and cash buyers work pipelines of motivated or forced sellers (pre-foreclosure, lender and court sales, estates, life events), and firms sell lead scores that predict who will sell, not who will sell cheaply. Published evidence that these scores work is thin, a single independent test showing about one flagged owner in five selling within six months, and "distress" or "motivated" labels in listings are not reliable evidence of a discount.

### Cited Findings
- The buyers who obtain large discounts are repeat professionals: the most experienced group in Los Angeles bought about 18-21% below market, more in cold markets, held for about two quarters and earned most of their return at purchase from "(presumably) desperate or 'motivated' sellers"; inexperienced investors bought at about market. [FT] — [Bayer et al., NBER WP 16784](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16784/w16784.pdf)
- The traditional channel as described in the iBuyer paper: "traditional cash buyer intermediaries often target distressed properties, such as foreclosures, short sales, or homes in need of renovations", and "finding a cash buyer involves costly search". [FT] — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252, footnote 16](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- Insiders' edge is information about supply and history: agents' 2.54% purchase discount rests on knowing available listings and previous purchase prices, and on bargaining power. [ABS] — [Agarwal, He, Sing & Song (2019)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v134y2019i3p715-735.html)
- Likelihood-to-sell scoring, the one independent test found: Altos Research checked a 5% sample of one vendor's 2017 "likely mover" leads against closed sales and found 19.5% sold within six months, described as "by far the best lead success rate we've seen in the real estate industry"; comparison rates in the article are about 1% for cold calling and about 10% for portal leads; the vendor's customers self-report 36.4%. The outcome is a sale, not a below-market sale. [FT] — [Inman, 26 March 2019](https://www.inman.com/2019/03/26/revaluates-likely-to-move-score-is-right-1-in-5-times/)
- What a vendor's sale-propensity score is built from, and how it is marketed: ownership length, estimated equity, mortgage age, liens, tax delinquency, pre-foreclosure notices, bankruptcies, divorce filings, vacancy, absentee or out-of-state ownership. The page claims "82% accuracy", that the score "identifies 64% of all properties that eventually sell", and that flagged properties are right "more than 6 out of 10 times", with no definition of the test set or time window and no independent validation cited. [FT, vendor marketing] — [BatchData, "The Science Behind Sale Propensity Scoring"](https://batchdata.io/blog/science-behind-sale-propensity-scoring)
- Listing language: agent remarks grouped into themes and priced with a hedonic model are mostly statistically significant; negative comments "are associated with lower sales prices", but so are some positive ones such as "new paint" and "good location", which the authors read as hype. The abstract gives no separate result for "motivated seller" wording. [ABS] — [Haag, Rutherford & Thomson (2000), "Real Estate Agent Remarks: Help or Hype?", Journal of Real Estate Research 20(1-2):205-216](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjerxx/v20y2000i1-2p205-216.html)
- Auctions and lender sales as channels: auction discounts of 0-9% in a boom and 9-21% in a bust after controlling for quality (Mayer 1998); bank-owned homes sell for less, but buyers' excess returns are small relative to transaction costs (Harding, Rosenblatt & Yao 2012); the assessors' standard regards lender sales as low because the lender "is highly motivated to sell and may be required by banking regulations to remove the property from its books". [ABS / FT] — [Mayer (1998)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i1p41-66.html); [Harding et al. (2012)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v71y2012i2p204-218.html); [IAAO sales verification standard, 5.4.4](https://iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Verification_Adjustment_of_Sales.pdf)
- Dubai, "distress" as a marketing label (2016): agencies advertised properties as distress sales in a slow market; one broker said "Too many agents advertise distressed sales" where the seller wants a quick sale at market price; another said some such listings were "non-existent"; an advertised example was about 10% off, against one broker's benchmark of roughly 17% for a true distressed sale; a portal's chief executive put distressed listings at "0.6 per cent of its total listings". [FT] — [The National, "Property agents bait Dubai homebuyers with 'distress' deals", 9 January 2016](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/property-agents-bait-dubai-homebuyers-with-distress-deals-1.177373)
- Scale and margin of the algorithmic model today: the surviving US iBuyer bought 1,706 homes in the fourth quarter of 2025 at a 1.0% contribution margin, which shows how little room algorithmic pricing leaves even with a seller-initiated pipeline. [FT] — [Opendoor Technologies, Q4 2025 earnings release](https://www.boardroomalpha.com/sec/open-8-k-2026-02-19-0001801169-26-000009/q42025formxex991earningsre.htm)

### Inferences
- In practice the valuation model is a pricing and triage tool that follows sourcing. The discount comes from the seller's circumstances and the buyer's speed, cash and access; the model's job is to say what the property is worth so that the buyer knows how much of a discount is on the table and which leads are not worth a visit.
- A screen that requires a sign of forced or urgent sale that does not depend on the residual (seller type, procedure, repeated cuts, vacancy) in addition to a low price matches how professionals work and how the validation standards read the data. The missing step for someone who wants to buy is to apply that pairing to properties on offer now, not to completed transfers.
- Lead scores predict the event "will sell", for which the base rate is a few percent a year and a five- to twenty-fold lift is useful to an agent seeking listings. They do not predict "will sell below market", and no evidence was found that they do. For an investor they narrow where to look; the discount still has to be negotiated and verified.
- Self-declared distress in a listing is weak evidence. The Dubai reporting and the remarks literature both suggest the label is used to attract enquiries; the test is the price against registry comparables for the same building and unit type, plus a verifiable reason for urgency.
- Realistic expectations from the literature: a few percent for informed insiders, 5-13% for active flippers depending on the cycle, about 20% for the most experienced specialists and for auctions in a weak market, with repair, holding and transaction costs to come off. The deep end of that range has only been documented for people doing this repeatedly and at scale.

### Gaps
- No independent or peer-reviewed validation was found of "motivated seller" or distress lead scores as predictors of purchase discount or investor return; all accuracy figures other than the Altos test are vendor claims.
- No audited evidence was found in this pass on prices achieved at Dubai property auctions (court enforcement sales, licensed auction houses) or on bank-distressed sales relative to registry comparables; other research strands may cover Dubai deal channels.
- Price-drop alerts: the only evidence opened is Knight (2002) on what price cuts signal; no study of alert-based buying was found.
- Published evidence on wholesalers and "we buy houses" operators (discounts paid, margins) was not searched.
- The Dubai "distress" article dates from 2016; 2026 reports of distress listings at 10-50% discounts circulating among agents are known only from search snippets and are unverified.
