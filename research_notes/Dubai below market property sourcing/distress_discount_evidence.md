## 1. Foreclosure / forced-sale discount: what the main studies find and how estimates change with controls

### Takeaway
Cross-sectional (hedonic) studies usually put the foreclosure/REO discount at roughly 10–28% in the US and 20–25% in Stockholm, with court-auction settings reaching 20–50% (Berlin) or more, but the estimates that control hardest for condition, selection and unobserved quality fall to about 5–8%, and one widely cited repeat-sales study concludes the implied discount is smaller than transaction costs. Apartments/condominiums show foreclosure discounts at least as large as houses; forced sales that are not foreclosures (bankruptcy, death/estate) are far smaller, about 3–9%.

### Cited Findings

**Campbell, Giglio and Pathak (2011)**
- [FT] Data: 1,783,360 arm's-length transactions in Massachusetts, 1987 to March 2008 (64.4% single-family, 11.1% multifamily, 24.5% condominiums); 5.6% of observations are forced sales (death-related 1.7%, bankruptcy-related 0.8%, foreclosure 3.1%). Published as American Economic Review 101(5): 2108–31; the version opened is NBER Working Paper 14866 (April 2009). — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866 full text](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf); citation details at [NBER abstract page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866)
- [FT] Hedonic regression with zip-code × year fixed effects, house characteristics and census-tract controls: a single "forced sale" dummy has a coefficient of −0.199, "corresponding to a price discount of ... 18%". Split by type the coefficients are −0.051 (death, seller under 70), −0.068 (death, seller 70+), −0.032 (bankruptcy) and −0.323 (foreclosure); "The coefficient for foreclosure implies a large price discount of 28%." (≈5.0%, 6.6%, 3.1% and 27.6% by my conversion.) — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [SEC] The published AER version is cited at 27%, and the range across its specifications at 22%–47%. — [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf); [FHFA Mortgage Market Note 12-01, Exhibit 1](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006)
- [FT] By property type (Table 6, Panel B), the foreclosure coefficient is −0.253 for single-family houses, −0.359 for multi-family houses and −0.322 for condominiums (≈22.4%, 30.2% and 27.5%, my conversion; N = 1,149,215 / 197,124 / 437,021). "Overall and foreclosure-related discounts are larger for condominiums and multi-family houses, and smaller for single-family houses." — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [FT] Timing: the foreclosure coefficient is −0.315 for sales within one year of foreclosure, −0.452 for 1–2 years after and −0.472 for 2–3 years after (≈27%, 36%, 38%, my conversion); more than 80% of foreclosure-related sales happen within a year. — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [FT] Seller count: death-related discounts are much larger with a single seller (−0.082 young, −0.095 old) than with two sellers (−0.035, −0.053); bankruptcy-related −0.061 with one seller vs −0.014 with two; the largest bankruptcy coefficient (−0.053) is for sales within a year after filing. — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [ABS] The forced-sale discount is time-varying, "larger and more persistent when forced sales are a higher share of transactions"; foreclosure discounts are larger for low-priced houses in low-priced zip codes. — [NBER Digest, July 2009](https://www.nber.org/digest/jul09/forced-sales-and-house-prices)

**Clauretie and Daneshvary (2009)**
- [ABS] Real Estate Economics 37(1): 43–67. "Most previous empirical research estimates a greater than 20% discount associated with the sale of foreclosed properties"; after adding property condition and the marketing-time/price link and correcting for two kinds of spatial price interdependence, earlier estimates "are about one‐third higher than the true discount caused by foreclosure per se." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v37y2009i1p43-67.html)
- [SEC] Their corrected estimate is reported as 7.5% (hedonic, US, 2004–2007) and as 8% (Las Vegas, single-family, 2004–2007, hedonic and generalized two-stage). — [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf); [FHFA MMN 12-01, Exhibit 1](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006)

**Harding, Rosenblatt and Yao (2012)**
- [ABS] Journal of Urban Economics 71(2): 204–218. Compares holding-period returns of REO purchasers with purchasers of similar non-distressed homes: "the majority of REO purchasers do not earn economically significant excess returns"; "On average, the implied market discount is less than typical transaction costs"; "lenders are not irrationally dumping REO properties and REO investors are not reaping extraordinary profits." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v71y2012i2p204-218.html)
- [SEC] Reported numerically as hedonic 18–25% (2001–2009) versus repeat-sales 8.4% (1990–2008); and as 1%, 11%, 14% and 21% across Atlanta, Columbus, Las Vegas and Los Angeles (1990–2008, single-family). The secondary sources do not agree on sample details (one says 13 MSAs). — [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf); [FHFA MMN 12-01, Exhibit 1](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006); [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), section 5.3](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)

**Zhou, Yuan, Lako, Sklarz and McKinney (2015)**
- [ABS] Real Estate Economics 43(3): 683–718; 1.34 million REO sales in 16 CBSAs, 2000–2012; discount defined as REO sale price relative to a normal-sale estimated market value. Findings: "a high REO sale concentration in a market increases the foreclosure discount"; the discount "is negatively related to recent house-price appreciation"; "the often reported high foreclosure discount for lower value properties is likely due to property condition." The abstract notes that inconsistent definitions give discounts "that vary from nonexistent to sizeable". — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v43y2015i3p683-718.html)
- [SEC] Headline estimate reported as 14.7%. — [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)

**Donner, Song and Wilhelmsson (2016); Donner (2017) — Stockholm, apartments**
- [ABS] Journal of Housing Economics 34: 60–68; sold apartments and single-family houses in Stockholm, 2006–2013; hedonic spatial Durbin model: "a negative impact on transaction price of 20.1% for foreclosed apartments and 24.6% for foreclosed single-family houses"; the discount "increases when the number of attempted auctions is limited", reaching 29.1% for apartments forfeited because of lost housing-association membership. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jhouse/v34y2016icp60-68.html)
- [ABS] Donner (2017), KTH Working Paper 17/2: using individual appraisals of foreclosed apartments sold at auction in Stockholm, "A mean discount of 7.9% with a corresponding median value of 9.5%"; "about half of the impact on price caused by a forced sale is attributable to the search process". — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/kthrec/2017_002.html)

**Daminger and Wiersma (2024) — Berlin, apartments only**
- [FT] WIFO Working Paper 675/2024 (not peer reviewed). All Berlin residential transactions 1984–2022 from the official appraiser committee; final sample 391,420 apartment transactions, of which 11,137 (2.8%) are court foreclosure auctions. Static hedonic discount 39%; year-by-year discount ranges from −50.9% (2004) to −8.6% (2018) and was −19.1% in 2022 (e.g. 2008 −43.9%, 2012 −21.1%, 2016 −14.8%, 2020 −20.5%). Foreclosures were up to 8.8% of all apartment sales (2004). — [Daminger & Wiersma, WIFO WP 675](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [SEC] The only other German study (Just et al. 2019, all German foreclosures 2008–2011 against asking prices) estimates 19–25.5%. — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), section 5.3](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)

**More recent work and reviews**
- [ABS] Conklin, Coulson, Diop and Mota (2023), Journal of Urban Economics 134: "Current research documents astonishingly large price discounts for foreclosures and short sales. However, such outsized estimates may largely be due to omitted variables bias." Using appraisal fixed effects (appraisers match subject and comparable on observed and unobserved attributes) they obtain "foreclosure and short sale discounts of approximately 5% after controlling for a rich set of characteristics", "attributable mostly to the stigma associated with distress itself." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v134y2023ics0094119023000153.html)
- [ABS] Review article — Cohen, Coughlin and Yao (2016), Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Review 98(3): 159–188: "Estimates of the own foreclosure discount generally range from near zero to 28 percent, with most estimates greater than 12 percent." Spillover discounts on nearby houses "are generally less than 2 percent and diminish rapidly with distance." — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlrv:00060)
- [FT] Regulator primer — Patrabansh, FHFA Mortgage Market Note 12-01 (17 Sep 2012): "While the academic estimates range from zero to 50 percent depending on location, time and controls used in the econometric models, the majority of estimates of REO discount in the academic literature are in the 10 to 25 percent range". Raw media/vendor figures are much higher (RealtyTrac 33%–41% in 2010–2012; a 2011 Harris survey found US adults expected to pay 38% less for a foreclosed house). — [FHFA MMN 12-01](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006)
- [SEC] Other estimates tabulated in opened sources: Shilling, Benjamin & Sirmans (1990) 24% (Baton Rouge condominiums, 1985); Forgey, Rutherford & VanBuskirk (1994) 23% (Arlington TX); Hardin & Wolverton (1996) 22% (Phoenix apartments, 1993–94); Springer (1996) 4–6% (Arlington TX); Carroll, Clauretie & Neill (1997) 0–14% (Las Vegas); Pennington-Cross (2006) 22% (US repeat sales, 1995–99); Sumell (2009) 50% (Cuyahoga County OH); Anenberg & Kung (2014) 16%; Chinloy, Hardin & Wu (2017) 15.3% REO and 19.0% auction (US, 2010–13). — [FHFA MMN 12-01, Exhibit 1](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006); [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [ABS] Allen, Benefield, Cain and Maynard (2024), Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 68(2): 318–353: discounts for short sales and lender-owned properties "differed significantly early in the crisis" but were "relatively similar" by the end of the sample; distressed properties "stayed on the market significantly longer than non-distressed properties", contrary to earlier findings. No magnitudes on the page. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrefec/v68y2024i2d10.1007_s11146-022-09911-2.html)
- No meta-analysis of foreclosure discounts was found; the closest are the two reviews above and the tables in Davids (2025) and FHFA (2012). — [search result summary, EconPapers listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlrv:00060)

**Outside the US**
- [ABS] Hong Kong — Chau and Ng (2008), International Journal of Property Sciences 1(1), hedonic model on Hong Kong residential data: "foreclosed properties are sold at a 10% discount in an up market, but are sold at no discount in a down market", explained by bank incentives (in a boom the loan is below value and the bank sells fast; in a slump a low price crystallises a loss). — [journal page](https://ejournal.um.edu.my/index.php/IJPS/article/view/4612)
- [ABS] Italy — Loberto (2021), Bank of Italy, SUERF Policy Brief 89 (based on Temi di discussione 1325): about 750,000 dwellings listed on the largest Italian portal in ~100 cities, 2016H2–2018: "I estimate that the foreclosure discount is approximately between 42 and 56 percent", "higher for smaller dwellings and those in suburban neighborhoods"; listings within 150 m of a foreclosure are 1.1% lower. Based on listing/base prices, not matched sale prices. — [SUERF Policy Brief 89](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/24415/foreclosures-and-house-prices)
- [ABS] China — Qu and Huang (2024), Habitat International, 11 cities, July 2017–October 2020 online judicial auctions: "an average economic depreciation for China's judicial auction housing market of approximately 14%", larger when purchase risk and market uncertainty are higher. — [author's publication page](https://maggiehyq.github.io/publication/judicial-auction)
- [FT] South Africa — Davids (18 Dec 2025 working paper, University of Cape Town), Cape Town freehold properties 2011–2018, 2,497 foreclosed sales (1.7% of transactions), auctions without reserve prices: raw hedonic auction discount 56.1%; 14.5% once buyer-borne costs are added; repeat-sales 30.2–31.4%. Properties with a foreclosure notice sold before the auction: 8.7% discount; sold after a suspended auction: 12%. — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [PRESS] UK — lender-services firm HML, reported 6 Aug 2014: repossessed homes sold "around 35% below market value" in the six years after the financial crisis (London 78% of market value; Scotland/North West/North 63%). No sample or method given; HML itself attributes the gap partly to "the deteriorating condition of empty repossessed properties". — [YourMoney.com](https://www.yourmoney.com/mortgages/repossessed-properties-sell-for-35-below-true-value/)

### Inferences
- The same phenomenon is measured three ways with very different answers: (i) raw comparison with average prices (33–55%, media and vendor figures), (ii) hedonic regression with location and size controls (roughly 10–28% US, 20–25% Stockholm), (iii) designs that control for condition or unobserved quality (5–8%). A reader should treat (iii) as the best estimate of what a buyer gains on a like-for-like US REO purchase and (ii) as an upper bound.
- Court-auction regimes (Berlin 20–50%, Stockholm 20%, Cape Town 14.5–31% net of costs, Italy 42–56% on listing data, China ~14%) produce larger measured discounts than US bank-owned resales, and the gap tracks auction design (reserve rules, inspection, cash-only payment) more than property type.
- Apartment evidence does not support the idea that apartments are immune: condominiums show a 27.5% foreclosure coefficient in Massachusetts, apartments 20.1% in Stockholm and 20–50% in Berlin. What apartments do escape is the maintenance-driven part (death-related discount ≈1% for condos vs 6–8% for houses), because the building is collectively maintained.
- Discounts move with the cycle in both directions: larger when forced sales are a big share of the market (Campbell et al.; Zhou et al.; Berlin 2000–2009), smaller in booms (Berlin 2016–2018 at 9–15%); but where the seller is a bank that books a loss, the opposite can hold (Hong Kong: 10% in up markets, none in down markets).

### Gaps
- Exact tables of Clauretie & Daneshvary (2009), Harding, Rosenblatt & Yao (2012) and Zhou et al. (2015) were paywalled; their numbers above come from other papers' literature tables and those tables disagree in detail (e.g. Harding et al. given as 8.4%, as 18–25%, and as 1–21% by MSA).
- No peer-reviewed like-for-like estimate was found for the UK, Spain, Singapore or any Gulf market. UK figures found are lender or auction-industry averages without controls.
- The Shanghai finding that judicial-auction homes moved "from premium to discount" after a 2021 purchase-restriction change appeared only in a search snippet (Finance Research Letters 2025) and was not opened. [UNVERIFIED]
- The St. Louis Fed review and the Fannie Mae working-paper version of Conklin et al. could not be opened in full (redirect / HTTP 403), so their study-by-study tables are not reproduced here.

## 2. Auctions: discount or premium relative to negotiated sales, and what it depends on

### Takeaway
Where auctions are a mainstream, voluntary sale method (Melbourne, Sydney, Christchurch, Dublin) they sell at par or at a premium; where they are used for distressed or court-ordered disposals they sell at a discount that widens in weak markets (Mayer: 0–9% in a boom, 9–21% in a bust), with naive hedonic estimates overstating it because auctioned stock is of lower quality. Court-run auctions with restricted inspection, cash payment and thin, repeat bidders show the largest discounts; widening participation (electronic bidding) measurably narrows them.

### Cited Findings

**Mayer (1998)**
- [ABS] Real Estate Economics 26(1): 41–66. Repeat-sales method "to control for unobserved differences in the quality of auction properties": "Properties auctioned in Los Angeles during the 1980s boom sold at an estimated discount of 0%–9%, while sales in Dallas following the oil bust obtained discounts of 9%–21%", consistent with a model in which "the auction discount increases in downturns when a seller trades‐off a longer expected selling time in a search market against an immediate auction sale." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i1p41-66.html)
- [FT] Working-paper version (Federal Reserve Bank of Boston WP 93-1): hedonic estimates put minimum-price auctions at a 6% discount in Los Angeles and 17% in Dallas; for Dallas unpublished-reserve auctions the discount "falls from 31 percent to 23 percent" when the weighted repeat-sales equation replaces the hedonic one; scattered-site minimum-price auctions about 10% in Los Angeles vs 21% in Dallas; Los Angeles single-site (new condominium project) auctions show a 3.5% premium, not statistically significant. — [Boston Fed WP 93-1](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp93_1.pdf)
- [FT] Mayer on earlier US studies: Wright (1989) found HUD auctions brought "63 to 86 percent of the price obtained using broker contracts" (average discount 25%) and Gau, Quan & Sternberg (1990) a 33% discount on Austin land, but "previous papers that use a hedonic equation suffer from a selection bias problem, pushing auction coefficients towards finding larger discounts" because institutions choose "less desirable and harder to sell" units for auction. — [Boston Fed WP 93-1](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp93_1.pdf)
- [FT] Seller's side of the trade-off: holding costs for unoccupied units "can easily add up to between 1 and 2 percent per month" (interest, taxes, depreciation, insurance, marketing), and average selling times "can exceed a year for some types of property". — [Boston Fed WP 93-1](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp93_1.pdf)

**Voluntary-auction markets: Lusht (1996), Dotzour, Moorhead and Winkler (1998) and related**
- [ABS] Lusht (1996), Real Estate Economics 24(4): 517–530: "In an active market for middle‐ to high‐priced houses, auctions extracted higher prices than private negotiations." (Melbourne; magnitude not on the page.) — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:reesec:v:24:y:1996:i:4:p:517-530)
- [SEC] Mayer reports the earlier Lusht (1990) Melbourne result as private-listing prices 6.5% lower than auction prices (significant at 5%), in a data set with condition variables. — [Boston Fed WP 93-1](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp93_1.pdf)
- [ABS] Dotzour, Moorhead and Winkler (1998), Journal of Real Estate Research 16(1): 57–72, Christchurch, hedonic: "in some cases auctions can result in premium sale prices. In none of the cases studied did auctions result in lower prices than private-treaty sales." The abstract notes that in the US auctions are "often associated with distressed sales". — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/jre/issued/v16n11998p57-72.html)
- [SEC] A later review states that Dotzour et al. examined 5,344 Christchurch transactions and found a significant premium in two of four submarkets; that Newell et al. (1993) found the median Sydney auction price 3.6% above private treaty; that Stevenson & Young (2004) found a premium in Ireland; and that foreclosure auctions in Singapore (Ong, Lusht & Mak 2005) and court auctions in Taiwan (Lin, Tsai & Chang 1997; Lin & Huang 2005) show discounts. — [Susilawati & Lin, PRRES conference paper on Brisbane auctions](https://www.prres.org/uploads/813/1539/Susiliwati_Analysis_Auction_Market_in_Brisbane.pdf)
- [ABS] Allen and Swisher (2000), Journal of Real Estate Research 20(3): 279–298, one HUD auction: "sample properties sell at a significant discount relative to predicted market values and ... prices tend to increase as the auction proceeds, holding quality of the properties constant." (No magnitude on the page.) — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjerxx/v20y2000i3p279-298.html)
- [ABS] Failed auctions leave a mark: Cortés, Singh, Solomon and Strahan (2022, NBER WP 30760), Australia, where about a third of properties sell at auction: "properties that fail auctions sell later for a 2.6% discount", larger after multiple failures or no bids; "most of the pricing discounts reflect stigma". — [NBER abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30760)

**Judicial / foreclosure auctions**
- [FT] Germany (Berlin): court-run open-outcry English auctions; at the first auction date the highest bid must be at least half of the appraised market value (5/10 limit) and a lower-ranking creditor can reject a bid below 7/10 of appraised value; both limits apply only to the first date. Creditors generally do not bid, so the discount is a pure auction discount (20–50% over 1984–2022). The authors attribute part of it to the format: "weekday timing, the physical presence requirement, and the lack of prior inspection, which may complicate financing", a "limited pool of bidders, who are mostly specialized investors", and possible bidding rings (anecdotal). — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024)](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [SEC] In the US, discounts are significantly larger in states that require a judicial (court) foreclosure process (Mian, Sufi & Trebbi 2015; Cordell & Lambie-Hanson 2016). — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), footnote 21](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [ABS] Sweden: 20.1% for foreclosed apartments, rising to 29.1% where the number of auction attempts is limited; against individual appraisals the mean auction discount is 7.9%. — [Donner, Song & Wilhelmsson (2016)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jhouse/v34y2016icp60-68.html); [Donner (2017)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/kthrec/2017_002.html)
- [FT] South Africa (no reserve prices until 2018): private buyers at sheriff auctions paid 56.1% less than comparable sales and lenders buying in 58.7% less; after a failed first auction the reserve is set at the appraised value and cut to 80% at the next round (description of the post-2019 regime); only 3.8% of foreclosed properties were bought by the lender. — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [ABS] Italy and China: 42–56% (listing-based) and ~14% respectively; in Italy until 2015 buyers "had to pay in a single installment without mortgage access". — [Loberto, SUERF Policy Brief 89](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/24415/foreclosures-and-house-prices); [Qu & Huang (2024)](https://maggiehyq.github.io/publication/judicial-auction)
- [ABS] Participation matters causally: Mazzola (Erasmus University; SUERF Policy Brief 555, 30 Mar 2023), staggered adoption of online bidding by Florida counties, tax-roll data 2009–2019: "electronic bidding leads to a 5 percentage point increase in foreclosure auction success" (28% relative to the mean) and a "3 percentage point decline in auction discount" (45% relative to the mean); volume buyers and professional investors become a smaller share of winners. — [SUERF Policy Brief 555](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/64227/technology-and-asset-liquidations-evidence-from-real-estate-collateral)
- Gulf markets: no academic study of auction discounts was found; process facts and press evidence for Dubai are in section 8.

### Inferences
- Voluntary vs forced is the dividing line: the auction mechanism itself does not produce a discount (premiums of roughly 3.6–6.5% are reported where auctions are a normal marketing route and vendors choose them for sought-after stock); the discount comes from who sells (institutions and courts with high holding costs and no private information), what is sold (worse stock) and who can bid (cash buyers able to act without inspection).
- Mayer's own figures imply the naive Dallas discount (31%) contained about 8 points of quality selection; roughly three quarters of it survived repeat-sales control.
- Mazzola's baseline discount can be backed out from his own numbers (3 points = 45% of the mean, so a mean auction discount of about 6–7%; my arithmetic), which is far below the European court-auction figures and suggests US foreclosure auctions with active lender bidding leave little on the table.
- The stigma result for failed auctions (−2.6%) implies a property that has already failed one or more auction rounds is cheaper for a reason beyond its reserve cuts, and that failure is itself public information that later buyers price.

### Gaps
- The widely quoted "about 8% premium" for Lusht (1996), and its sample (163 auctions vs 58 or 80 private sales), appeared only in search snippets of secondary sources. [UNVERIFIED]
- The sample size of Dotzour et al. is given as 158 transactions in one search snippet and 5,344 in the review opened; not resolved.
- Allen & Swisher's discount magnitude, and Ong, Lusht & Mak's Singapore estimate, were not available on the pages opened.
- No study was found for Spain or for Hong Kong auctions specifically; no peer-reviewed study for any Gulf market.
- The job-market-paper version of Mazzola reports larger relative effects (27% and 42%) according to a search snippet; only the SUERF brief was opened.

## 3. Motivated, liquidity-constrained or time-pressured sellers: effect on price and time on market

### Takeaway
Measured urgency discounts for ordinary (non-foreclosure) sellers are mostly in the 3–12% range: about 3% for bankruptcy-related sales, 5–9% for estate sales (rising to 12.5–14% right before a legal deadline), 10–11% for self-declared highly motivated sellers in one survey study, about 3% plus fees for an instant sale to an iBuyer, and about 10–11% less from a cash buyer than from a mortgaged one. Financial stress short of foreclosure pushes the other way: owners with high loan-to-value ratios or nominal losses ask more, wait longer and sell for more.

### Cited Findings

**Glower, Haurin and Hendershott (1998)**
- [ABS] Real Estate Economics 26(4): 719–740, survey of home sellers: "a seller who, at the time of listing, has a planned date to move sells more quickly than one who does not"; "the shorter the planned time until a move at the time of listing, the shorter the actual duration of marketing time"; "seller motivation affects sale price, but not the list‐price markup." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v26y1998i4p719-740.html)
- [PRESS — author's university release, 24 Jan 1999] Sample: sellers who listed with one brokerage in Columbus, Ohio, 30 Apr 1990–31 Dec 1991, telephone survey. Highly motivated sellers (e.g. had already bought another home) were on the market "up to 30 percent less time than other sellers" and sold for "about 10 to 11 percent less than similar homes owned by less-motivated sellers"; they did not set a lower original list price ("It may be that highly motivated sellers start with the same list price, but they reduce it more quickly"). — [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/reasons-for-selling-house-may-affect-final-price-selling-time/)

**Springer (1996)**
- [ABS] Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 13(3): 237–254, single-family sales in Arlington, Texas, 1991–1993: "Results show selling price discounts for houses with sellers who are either eager, motivated, or anxious", for relocated sellers, foreclosures and vacant houses; "Only foreclosure houses show the reduced marketing time expected for properties with motivated sellers"; "the list price is the seller's primary mechanism for selling the property." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrefec/v13y1996i3p237-54.html)
- [SEC] Springer's foreclosure discount is tabulated as 4–6%. — [FHFA MMN 12-01, Exhibit 1](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006)

**Genesove and Mayer (1997, 2001); Anenberg (2011) — equity constraints and loss aversion**
- [ABS] Genesove and Mayer (1997), American Economic Review 87(3): 255–269, Boston condominiums, early 1990s: "An owner of a property with a high loan-to-value ratio sets a higher asking price, has a higher expected time on the market and, if he sells, receives a higher price than an owner with proportionately less debt." — [Hebrew University research portal abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/equity-and-time-to-sale-in-the-real-estate-market/); [NBER WP 4861](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4861)
- [ABS] Genesove and Mayer (2001), Quarterly Journal of Economics 116(4): 1233–1260, downtown Boston condominiums in the 1990s: owners facing nominal losses "set higher asking prices of 25-35 percent of the difference between the property's expected selling price and their original purchase price", "attain higher selling prices of 3-18 percent of that difference", and "exhibit a much lower sale hazard"; "The list price results are twice as large for owner-occupants as investors, but hold for both." — [NBER WP 8143](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8143)
- [ABS] Anenberg (2011), Regional Science and Urban Economics 41(1): 67–76, long panel from the San Francisco Bay Area: "owners facing nominal losses on their housing investments and owners with high LTV ratios sell for higher prices" and "the effects are larger than previously thought". (No magnitudes on the page.) — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/regeco/v41y2011i1p67-76.html)

**Estate, death and bankruptcy sales**
- [FT] Massachusetts: bankruptcy-related sales −0.032 (≈3%); −0.053 for sales within a year after filing; −0.061 with a single seller. The authors read these as "consistent with a true liquidity effect". Death-related sales −0.051 to −0.068 (≈5–7%), which they attribute mainly to poor maintenance, not urgency (see section 5). — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [PRESS — summary written by the authors, 2 Aug 2017] Andersen and Nielsen (2017), "Fire Sales and House Prices: Evidence from Estate Sales due to Sudden Death", Management Science 63(1): 201–212, Denmark, where estates must be settled within 12 months of death: "forced sales resulted in an average discount of 8.9%"; "Sales shortly after the sudden death occur at market prices", whereas sales "in the last three months before the deadline result in an average discount of 14%"; the average discount was 7.5% in years when prices rose 10% or more and 12.6% when prices fell. — [HKUST BizInsight](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2017/08/when-forced-sales-turn-fire-sales)

**The price of speed and certainty**
- [FT] UK Office of Fair Trading, Quick House Sales market study (OFT1499, August 2013): firms that buy within weeks "typically" pay such that sellers "forgo between 10 and 25 per cent of the market value of the property (although we have seen much higher figures on occasions)"; a common offer is "80 per cent of the full market value"; typical completion is "three to four weeks" against advertised "seven days"; the sector is one-half to one per cent of UK residential sales (£0.5–0.9 billion); sellers use it to "avoid repossession, clear debts or sort out financial problems", in relationship breakdown, or to move quickly; 70% of complaints came from vulnerable consumers. In complaints, late price cuts ranged "from 7 per cent to 53 per cent of the initial offer ... averaging 22 per cent". — [OFT1499](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/555de48540f0b669c400014b/oft1499.pdf)
- [FT] Buchak, Matvos, Piskorski and Seru (NBER WP 28252, revised June 2025): iBuyers let US households skip a listing process that averages 91 days; "They purchase properties at a 3.1 percentage point (pp) discount compared to similar listed homes" (about $9,000, 2013–2018), sell at about a 1.5% premium, and charge the seller a fee of about 6%; the discount fell to about 2.6% in 2018. They intermediate only "relatively liquid" and easy-to-value homes. — [NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- [PRESS — university release] Reher and Valkanov, "The Mortgage-Cash Premium Puzzle" (Journal of Finance): in about 2 million US sales 1980–2017 mortgaged buyers paid about 11% more than all-cash buyers; about 8% in 20,000+ Redfin sales and offers 2013–2021; in a survey experiment 3,000 homeowners would accept a mortgage offer only if it paid about 10% more; about 10% of mortgage-financed deals fail. "We find sellers are willing to leave money on the table to avoid the risk." — [UC San Diego Today](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/all-cash-home-buyers-pay-10-less-than-mortgage-buyers)
- [FT] Who captures urgency discounts: high-volume "middlemen" in Los Angeles buy at about 31% below expected market price versus 6% for low-volume flippers, and the authors attribute the steep discount to "locating 'good deals' and buying from 'motivated' sellers"; across all flippers the purchase discount is −0.206 log points in the cold market of 1992–98 against −0.071 in the 1999–2006 boom. — [Bayer, Geissler, Mangum & Roberts (2020), Review of Financial Studies 33(11)](https://sites.duke.edu/patrickbayer/files/2021/02/BGMR_RFS.pdf)
- [ABS] Institutional sellers are not uniformly urgent: Hong Kong banks sell foreclosed flats 10% below market in rising markets and at no discount in falling ones. — [Chau & Ng (2008)](https://ejournal.um.edu.my/index.php/IJPS/article/view/4612)

### Inferences
- For a private seller who is under time pressure but still controls the sale, the literature's central range is roughly 5–12% below what a patient seller would get, and it is strongly deadline-shaped: near zero when the seller still has months, 12.5–14% in the last weeks. Discounts of 15% or more belong to sellers who have lost control of the process (foreclosure, court auction) or who sell to a cash intermediary in days or weeks (10–25%).
- High leverage and paper losses are signs of a seller who will resist a low price, not accept one; that only reverses when a lender or court takes over, or a hard deadline arrives. This is the single most counter-intuitive result for a deal-sourcing strategy built on "owners who need liquidity".
- Cash and speed are themselves worth about 10% to US sellers; a buyer who can close fast without finance is being paid for certainty, which is separate from any distress on the seller's side.
- Investors are measurably less loss-averse than owner-occupiers (list-price effect half the size), so investor-owned stock should reprice faster in a downturn.

### Gaps
- Magnitudes in Glower et al. come from the university's press release, not the paper's tables; the 1997 Genesove–Mayer magnitudes (effect of 100% vs 80% loan-to-value) and Anenberg's (2011) magnitudes could not be confirmed on any page opened.
- Springer's sample size (2,317 transactions, May 1991–June 1993) appeared only in a search snippet. [UNVERIFIED]
- Benefield, Rutherford and Allen (2012, JREFE 45(4): 965–981; 180,921 sold properties) reportedly find estate sales sell at a discount and faster; seen only in a search snippet, no magnitude. [UNVERIFIED] A 12.5% figure for Andersen & Nielsen's near-deadline sales (working-paper version) was also seen only in a snippet. [UNVERIFIED]
- No study isolating divorce or job-loss sellers was found; Springer lumps them into "motivated". No quantitative study of corporate-relocation sellers was opened.

## 4. Signals observable before a sale: which indicators predict a discounted sale or a motivated seller

### Takeaway
The signals with measured effects are legal-process markers (a foreclosure notice: 9–12% below comparable sales even when sold before or outside the auction; a failed auction: −2.6%), deadlines (estate sales close to a statutory deadline: −14%), stale or repeatedly re-priced listings, vacancy, institutional or single sellers, and the word "motivated" in listing remarks (−4% to −8%, with longer, not shorter, selling time). Negative equity alone predicts a higher asking price, and in Dubai the "distress" label is used so loosely that it carries little information.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Foreclosure notice / scheduled auction: in Cape Town, properties that received a foreclosure notice sold 23.3–27.7% below comparable properties in the same suburb and year overall; split by route, 8.7% below when sold before the auction, 12% when sold after a suspended auction (shrinking to zero for sales more than a year after the auction date), against 56.1% at the auction itself. 29% of foreclosed properties sold before the auction date at prices nearly double the auction average. — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [FT] Payment-arrears trail: 59% of foreclosed properties had an administrative/billing-related municipal service request in the 12 months before sale against 15% of other properties, while condition-related complaints occurred "at almost identical rates"; buyers at auction inherit unpaid property taxes. — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [PRESS — authors' summary] Statutory deadline: Danish estate sales are at market prices shortly after the death and 14% below in the last three months before the 12-month settlement deadline. — [Andersen & Nielsen, HKUST BizInsight](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2017/08/when-forced-sales-turn-fire-sales)
- [ABS] Failed auction: −2.6% on the later sale, larger after several failures or no bids. — [Cortés, Singh, Solomon & Strahan (2022), NBER WP 30760](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30760)
- [ABS] List-price reductions: Knight (2002), Real Estate Economics 30(2): 213–237: "Homes most likely to undergo list price changes are those with high initial markups and vacant homes"; "Homes with large percentage changes in list price take longer to sell and ultimately sell at lower prices"; "mispricing the home in the initial listing is costly to the seller in both time and money." (No magnitudes on the page.) — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v30y2002i2p213-237.html)
- [ABS] Days on market and relisting: Tucker, Zhang and Zhu (2013), RAND Journal of Economics 44(2): 337–360: after Massachusetts stopped sellers resetting days-on-market by relisting in 2006, affected homes "experienced a $16,000 reduction in sale price" relative to Rhode Island; "Slow-moving homes suffered a greater reduction". — [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/88161)
- [PRESS — Los Angeles Times article reprinted 17 Dec 2006, reporting three academic studies] Listing-remark keywords: (a) Rutherford and co-authors, almost 60,000 closed sales in Tarrant County, Texas, 1994–97: homes described as having "motivated" sellers stayed on the market 15% longer and sold for 4% less, which Rutherford attributes to sellers who "overpriced the house to start with and eventually had to lower it"; (b) Anglin (University of Guelph), more than 20,000 Canadian listings 1997–2000: "motivated" listings sold for 8% less and took 15% longer, "moving" 1% less, "good-value" 5% less, "handyman special" sold in about half the average time; (c) an NBER study found "foreclosure", "as-is" and "handyman special" linked to "substantially lower sales prices". — [The Spokesman-Review / Los Angeles Times](https://spokesman.com/stories/2006/dec/17/motivated-seller-just-dont-say-so)
- [ABS] Haag, Rutherford and Thomson (2000), Journal of Real Estate Research 20(1-2): 205–216: MLS remarks grouped into themes give information on seller motivation; "Negative comments are associated with lower sales prices", and some positive comments ("new paint", "good location") are also associated with lower prices. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjerxx/v20y2000i1-2p205-216.html)
- [ABS] Vacancy: Turnbull and Zahirovic-Herbert (2011), Real Estate Economics 39(1): 19–43, 20-year transaction series: "The robust vacancy effects on price and liquidity across all market phases primarily reflect greater seller holding cost" and weaker bargaining power; stigma appears only in rising markets. Springer (1996) also finds a discount for vacant houses. (No magnitudes on the pages.) — [University of Memphis repository abstract](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/11721); [Springer (1996) abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrefec/v13y1996i3p237-54.html)
- [ABS] Tenant-occupied stock: Turnbull and Zahirovic-Herbert (2012), Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 44(3): 275–297: "strong transitory renter effects on both value and liquidity", no lasting penalty once the unit is no longer rented. (No magnitudes on the page.) — [University of Memphis repository abstract](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/11700)
- [FT] Number and type of sellers: single-seller estate and bankruptcy sales carry roughly double the discount of two-seller sales (−0.082/−0.095 vs −0.035/−0.053; −0.061 vs −0.014); high-volume flippers obtain part of their discount "by buying from nonoccupant sellers, who presumably have greater holding costs"; 26.6% of middlemen's purchases were previously foreclosed properties against 12.0% for owner-occupiers (36.8% vs 17.7% in cold markets). — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf); [Bayer et al. (2020)](https://sites.duke.edu/patrickbayer/files/2021/02/BGMR_RFS.pdf)
- [ABS] High loan-to-value or negative equity is not a discount signal while the owner controls the sale: such owners list higher, wait longer and obtain higher prices. — [Genesove & Mayer (1997)](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/equity-and-time-to-sale-in-the-real-estate-market/); [Genesove & Mayer (2001), NBER WP 8143](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8143)
- [ABS] Market-level signal: discounts widen where distressed sales are a large share of local transactions and where recent appreciation is weak. — [Zhou et al. (2015)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v43y2015i3p683-718.html); [NBER Digest on Campbell et al.](https://www.nber.org/digest/jul09/forced-sales-and-house-prices)
- [PRESS] Dubai listing labels: a portal chief executive said distressed listings rose in the second half of 2015 but were only 0.6% of total listings ("Distressed listings are few and far between"); one brokerage found three of its listings shown as "distressed" on a small portal that were neither distressed nor current; a sales director said a true distress sale would be about Dh500,000 off a Dh3m market value, while a seller who says "quick sale" often still wants market price. — [The National, 9 Jan 2016](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/property-agents-bait-dubai-homebuyers-with-distress-deals-1.177373)
- [PRESS — broker] A Dubai brokerage lists as seller-side signals: leverage, off-plan investors needing to exit near handover, urgent personal liquidity needs, legal or partnership disputes; it adds that "not every 'urgent sale' listing is below market" and that genuine opportunities often "never remain long on public portals". — [Engel & Völkers Dubai, updated 11 Sep 2026](https://www.engelvoelkers.com/ae/en/resources/distressed-properties-dubai)

### Inferences
- Ranked by how well the evidence supports them as predictors of a genuinely low price: (1) a legal or contractual deadline with a known date (court auction date, estate settlement, handover payment); (2) a lender or court already in the process (notice issued, auction scheduled, earlier auction failed); (3) a long listing history with repeated cuts; (4) vacancy combined with an institutional, estate or single seller; (5) remark keywords. Leverage and arrears data identify who is under pressure but, on the evidence, not who will sell cheaply until one of (1)–(2) applies.
- "Motivated" in a listing is a lagging indicator of earlier over-pricing: the home sells for 4–8% less but takes 15% longer, so it marks a seller who has already spent time, not one who will accept the first low bid.
- No study opened reports classification accuracy (hit rate, precision) for any of these signals; each is a conditional mean effect. A signal with a −4% to −12% average effect cannot by itself identify sales 15% below value.
- The Cape Town pattern (9–12% before or after the auction, 56% at it, of which three quarters is cost and risk) suggests the best risk-adjusted entry point is a negotiated purchase from an owner who has received a notice, before the auction, where normal inspection, title checks and finance are still possible.

### Gaps
- No quantitative evidence was found for absentee or multiple ownership, tax or service-charge arrears as predictors of price (only as markers of distress in Cape Town), or for mortgage-default notices in markets other than South Africa.
- Goodwin, Waller and Weeks (2014) reportedly find that, after correcting for self-selection, "motivated", "price reduced" and "vacant" have a positive effect on price and days on market, contradicting Haag et al.; seen only in a search snippet. [UNVERIFIED]
- Knight (2002), Turnbull & Zahirovic-Herbert (2011, 2012) and Tucker et al. (2013) magnitudes for slow-moving or vacant homes were not on the pages opened.
- Dubai has no public listing-history or default-notice data set that was located in this run; the 0.6% figure is a portal executive's statement from 2016.

## 5. Quality versus price: how much of the naive discount is a genuine discount

### Takeaway
Between half and four fifths of the naive foreclosure discount disappears once condition, unobserved quality and buyer-borne costs are accounted for in the US and South African evidence, leaving about 5–15%; the residual is attributed to seller urgency, buyer risk and stigma. The exception is court-auctioned apartments in Berlin, where quick resales show the discount is largely not quality, though buyers there bid without inspection and returns are measured before costs.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Death-related discounts are maintenance, not urgency: they do not depend on the timing of the sale relative to the death (significant even more than three years away), rise with seller age, are largest for single-family houses and "very small for condominiums", and are larger when the structure is a bigger share of value. "This confirms the suspicion that the estimated price effect is not directly related to the urgency of the sale, but results from unobserved poor maintenance." — [Campbell, Giglio & Pathak, NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [FT] Foreclosure discounts "appear to be related both to the urgency of sale, and to vandalism"; they are larger for cheap houses in low-priced areas, consistent with "fixed costs of protection that justify larger proportional discounts on cheaper houses". The authors lack time-varying condition data (characteristics measured once, in 2007). — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [FT] No lasting effect on the property: controlling for general price persistence, "we do not find that forced sales have large dynamic effects" on the next sale price; a previous death-related sale is followed by a modest positive effect, as if the next owner rectified the maintenance. — [NBER WP 14866](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14866/w14866.pdf)
- [ABS] Earlier (>20%) estimates are "about one‐third higher than the true discount caused by foreclosure per se" once property condition and marketing time are included; corrected estimate 7.5–8% [SEC]. — [Clauretie & Daneshvary (2009) abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v37y2009i1p43-67.html); [Davids (2025), Table 9](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [ABS] With appraisal fixed effects and controls "including quality and condition", foreclosure and short-sale discounts are "approximately 5%", "attributable mostly to the stigma associated with distress itself". — [Conklin, Coulson, Diop & Mota (2023)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v134y2023ics0094119023000153.html)
- [ABS] "the often reported high foreclosure discount for lower value properties is likely due to property condition." — [Zhou et al. (2015)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/reesec/v43y2015i3p683-718.html)
- [FT] Mayer: hedonic auction estimates are biased by selection of poorer stock; repeat sales cut the Dallas unpublished-reserve discount from 31% to 23%, and even then "Some of these properties may have been in poor shape after having been previously occupied by owners who were evicted." — [Boston Fed WP 93-1](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp93_1.pdf)
- [FT] FHFA lists six explanations for measured REO discounts — condition (damage, deferred maintenance, abandonment, stripping by the former owner), characteristics (older, smaller homes), market (worse locations and timing), buyer risk aversion and hidden repair or title costs, seller holding costs, and stigma — and concludes "estimates of REO discount are larger than the true discount because the estimates are also capturing difference in quality or condition of property"; three cited papers "all argue that the pure stigma effect is likely small". — [FHFA MMN 12-01](https://www.fhfa.gov/media/33006)
- [FT] Cape Town decomposition: adding municipal condition-complaint controls leaves the auction discount "virtually unchanged" although those controls are powerful (a property with any structural or general condition complaint sells for 70.3% less); adding the auctioneer's commission, two years of property-tax arrears and a contested eviction cuts the discount "more than threefold" from 56.1% to 14.5%; "a substantial portion of the raw auction discount can be rationalised by the transaction costs and risks borne by buyers". Eviction "in many cases" takes "up to and over one year". — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [FT] Berlin apartments: flipping a foreclosed apartment within one year yields 62.1% against 30.5% for matched non-distressed apartments with the same holding period, which "suggests that there is a large discount for foreclosed properties in Berlin that is not related to housing quality"; and apartments once foreclosed show no lower appreciation in later regular sales (coefficient 0.000–0.001 with full fixed effects), so "the foreclosure discount is likely restricted to the transaction itself and does not impose a lasting stigma". The authors caution that unrecorded renovations cannot be excluded and that quick resales may be the cases where feared poor condition "did not turn out to be true". — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024)](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [FT] Los Angeles flippers: using a design that tracks whether above-market resale prices persist to the next sale, "flippers are not investing much more than 2% of a house's value beyond the normal transaction's 'sprucing up' for sale"; "unobserved investment is not the primary driver of the purchase discount". — [Bayer et al. (2020)](https://sites.duke.edu/patrickbayer/files/2021/02/BGMR_RFS.pdf)
- [FT] iBuyers: "The simultaneous buy discount and sell premium helps rule out the alternative explanation that iBuyers' purchase discount simply reflects houses with worse unobservable characteristics." — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf)
- [ABS] Stockholm: "about half of the impact on price caused by a forced sale is attributable to the search process". — [Donner (2017)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/kthrec/2017_002.html)
- [FT] How valuers treat distressed comparables: in a US appraisal data set, distressed comparables are adjusted upwards by only 1.6–2.4 percentage points more than other comparables within the same appraisal, and appraisals using distressed comparables come in 8–10% lower before controlling for location; the authors conclude adjustments "are not large enough". — [Conklin, Coulson & Diop, "Distressed Comps" (Dec 2021 draft; later Real Estate Economics 51(1))](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2022/preliminary/paper/B4N3kHnG)

### Inferences
- Share of the naive discount that survives, from the figures above (my arithmetic): Mayer about 74% (23 of 31 points); Clauretie & Daneshvary about 35–40% (7.5–8 of ">20"); Harding et al. about a third to a half (8.4 against 18–25, on Davids' tabulation); Conklin et al. about a fifth to two fifths (5 against 12–28); Cape Town hedonic about a quarter (14.5 of 56.1). A working rule of "roughly one third to one half of a hedonic discount is real" fits the US evidence; in auction settings with heavy buyer costs the surviving share is lower.
- For apartments the maintenance channel is weak (collective upkeep), so the quality component should be smaller than for houses; the remaining non-price explanations are legal and occupancy risk (tenant or former owner in possession, arrears that pass to the buyer, no inspection) rather than physical neglect.
- A valuation model's large negative residual on a completed sale has the same identification problem as a hedonic foreclosure dummy: it mixes unobserved quality (view, floor, condition, tenancy), non-arm's-length pricing and true urgency. The literature's upper bound for pure urgency among ordinary sellers (about 10–14%) sits below a 15% threshold, so a sale flagged at −15% or worse needs a second, residual-independent signal before it is read as distress; the studies above are consistent with requiring one.
- Forced-sale discounts are attached to the transaction, not the unit (Massachusetts, Berlin): the next sale is at normal prices. This is the mechanism by which a buyer profits, and also the reason a past forced-sale price is weak evidence of the unit's value.

### Gaps
- No study separates the discount for legal encumbrances or sitting tenants from the rest, apart from Davids' eviction-cost adjustment (an assumption applied uniformly, not observed).
- No evidence for Dubai or the Gulf on condition, service-charge arrears or tenancy as components of observed discounts.
- Lambie-Hanson (2015) on mortgage distress and maintenance is cited by several opened papers but was not itself opened.

## 6. Returns to buyers of distressed property after costs and risks

### Takeaway
Specialist, high-volume buyers earn large returns almost entirely from the purchase discount (Los Angeles middlemen: 31% discount, about 28% nominal per flip after about 12.6% transaction costs, held under six months), while ordinary and occasional buyers do not: the average US REO buyer earns no economically significant excess return, low-volume flippers earn mostly market appreciation, and flipping profits in Las Vegas fell to zero by 2007. Court-auction buyers in Berlin and Stockholm show much larger gross excess returns, but these are before costs, unlevered and not risk-adjusted.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Bayer, Geissler, Mangum and Roberts (2020), Review of Financial Studies 33(11): 5212–5247; Los Angeles area 1988–2012, 1,300,031 properties, 80,904 flips (resold within two years, purchases 1992–2009). All flippers: purchase 11% below and sale 7% above expected market value (coefficients −0.1152 and +0.0762); purchase discount −0.2063 in the cold market 1992–98, −0.0714 in the boom, −0.1859 after the 2006 peak. — [Bayer et al. (2020)](https://sites.duke.edu/patrickbayer/files/2021/02/BGMR_RFS.pdf)
- [FT] Sources of return by flipper volume (Table 4; buyer discount / seller premium / market growth / transaction cost / nominal return / quarters held / annualized return): Low volume (49,660 flips) −0.059 / 0.068 / 0.130 / −0.104 / 0.153 / 4.07 / 0.150; Med-low (17,892) −0.147 / 0.073 / 0.095 / −0.112 / 0.202 / 3.15 / 0.256; Med-high (5,473) −0.263 / 0.061 / 0.058 / −0.123 / 0.259 / 2.39 / 0.434; High (7,253) −0.312 / 0.058 / 0.037 / −0.126 / 0.281 / 1.93 / 0.581. (2020)](https://sites.duke.edu/patrickbayer/files/2021/02/BGMR_RFS.pdf)

Even after adjusting for opportunity costs, this translates to economic profits of nearly 20%. However ... by 2007, economic returns to a flip fall to 0." Estimates omit repair costs and agents' fees, so they "necessarily state the maximum profit of a flip". Flips reached 22% of all sales in 2004Q3. — [Depken, Hollans & Swidler, Chicago Fed seminar draft](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/others/research/research-calendar-attachments/seminars-2009/sem-swidler070709-pdf.pdf)
- [ABS] US REO buyers on average: "the majority of REO purchasers do not earn economically significant excess returns. On average, the implied market discount is less than typical transaction costs." — [Harding, Rosenblatt & Yao (2012)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v71y2012i2p204-218.html)
- [SEC] In the same paper only resales within a year outperform (excess annualized return 33.2%, based on 868 repeat-sale pairs), and with much higher risk. — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), section 5.3 and footnote 20](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [FT] Berlin apartments, matched repeat sales (20,408 pairs): buying at a foreclosure auction and reselling normally gives an annualized appreciation 20.5 percentage points above matched non-distressed pairs (22.6 without fixed effects; control group 8.4% a year). By holding period (annualized, foreclosure-then-regular vs control): up to 1 year 62.1% vs 30.5% (n = 1,415); 1–2 years 42.0% vs 21.7%; 2–3 years 26.4% vs 12.3%; 4–5 years 14.0% vs 4.1%; over 10 years 8.9% vs 1.6%. The owner who is foreclosed loses 9.6 points a year relative to controls. — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), Tables 4–5](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [FT] Caveats stated by the same authors: returns ignore transaction costs and "should be viewed as upper bounds"; they are unlevered; the premium "might reflect the higher ex-ante risk associated with foreclosure apartments, such as the limited or absent opportunity to inspect the apartment before the auction"; the buyer cannot resell until entered in the land register "several months later". — [Daminger & Wiersma (2024), section 6](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [SEC] Stockholm: foreclosure-then-regular repeat sales earn 37.8 to 48.6 percentage points more annualized than matched controls (mean holding period 4.3 years). — [Donner (2017) as reported in Daminger & Wiersma (2024)](https://wifo.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/upload-8272/wp_2024_675_.pdf)
- [FT] Cape Town: buyers at foreclosure auctions are disproportionately professionals (27% non-natural persons vs 8.2% for ordinary sales; 24.6% repeat buyers vs 8.5%) and flip quickly (26.7% resold within 6 months and 51.6% within 18 months, vs 1.0% and 4.7%); net of commission, tax arrears and eviction costs their purchase discount is 14.5% (hedonic) to 30–31% (repeat sales). — [Davids (2025)](https://allandav.github.io/papers/Davids_Foreclosure_Discounts.pdf)
- [FT] Intermediaries buying from ordinary sellers earn thin spreads: iBuyers' gross spread is about 5% (3.4% in 2018) on a median 105-day hold, before their own selling fee of about 6% and carrying costs; they avoid illiquid and hard-to-value homes. UK quick-sale providers pay 10–25% below market and told the regulator "market conditions have been tough since 2008 and profit margins are tight". — [Buchak et al., NBER WP 28252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28252/w28252.pdf); [OFT1499](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/555de48540f0b669c400014b/oft1499.pdf)
- [ABS] Wider participation removes part of the specialists' edge: after online bidding, volume buyers and professional investors are a smaller share of Florida foreclosure-auction winners and the discount falls by 3 points. — [Mazzola, SUERF Policy Brief 555](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/64227/technology-and-asset-liquidations-evidence-from-real-estate-collateral)
- [PRESS] Dubai anecdote: an Emirates Hills property bought at auction in late 2017 was resold within four months for a 15% profit, according to a brokerage chief executive; a 2011 auction buyer said "We will be re-selling the property as soon as we get the papers." — [Gulf News, 27 Sep 2019](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/cityscape-global-bargains-to-be-had-at-dubai-property-auctions-1.66721129); [Gulf News, "Forfeited freehold properties"](https://gulfnews.com/uae/forfeited-freehold-properties-going-going-gone-1.946918)

### Inferences
- The break-even discount is set by round-trip costs. In Los Angeles they were 10–13% of price; a buyer who captures the all-flipper average discount of 11% and resale premium of 7% clears only a few points before holding and capital costs, which is why low-volume flippers depend on market appreciation for 84% of their return.
- The returns that look exceptional come from a combination that occasional buyers rarely have: cash, volume, repeat presence at auctions, willingness to buy without inspection and with occupants, and speed of resale. Where that edge is competed away (US REO resales through brokers, online auctions), average excess returns are about zero.
- Returns are cyclical in the wrong direction for a naive strategy: discounts are deepest in cold markets, when resale is slowest and prices may still fall; Las Vegas flippers' returns went to zero as the market turned and neither group of Los Angeles investors anticipated the bust.
- For a hold-to-let buyer the Berlin table is the most relevant: the entry discount is worth about 7–10 points a year of extra appreciation even over 6–10+ year holds, gross of costs.

### Gaps
- No study of realised returns net of repairs, legal costs and failed deals was found for any auction market outside the US; the Berlin, Stockholm and Cape Town figures are price-based.
- No Dubai or Gulf evidence on returns beyond the two anecdotes above.
- LaCour-Little and Yang (2023), cited in the Berlin paper as finding that flips outperform most for distressed purchases, was not opened.

## 7. Definitions: market value versus forced sale in valuation standards

### Takeaway
Both the International Valuation Standards and the RICS Red Book define market value as a price between willing, knowledgeable parties "after proper marketing" and "without compulsion", and both say a forced sale is a description of the seller's situation, not a basis of value: its price cannot be estimated without knowing the specific constraint, bears only a coincidental relationship to market value, and confirmed forced-sale transactions are generally excluded from market-value evidence.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Market value (IVS, reproduced in the Red Book): "the estimated amount for which an asset or liability should exchange on the valuation date between a willing buyer and a willing seller in an arm's length transaction, after proper marketing and where the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently and without compulsion." "Without compulsion" means "each party is motivated to undertake the transaction, but neither is forced or unduly coerced to complete it." — [RICS Valuation – Global Standards (Red Book), effective 31 January 2022, incorporating IVS 104 paras 30.1–30.2](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/2021_11_25_rics_valuation_global_standards_effective_2022.pdf); definition also in the [IVSC glossary](https://ivsc.org/standards-glossary/)
- [FT] A falling market does not make a sale forced: "it is not necessarily imprudent for a seller to sell assets in a market with falling prices at a price that is lower than previous market levels." — [Red Book 2022 edition, IVS 104 para 30.2(h)](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/2021_11_25_rics_valuation_global_standards_effective_2022.pdf)
- [FT] IVS on forced sale (IVS 104 para 170 in the 2022 edition; IVS 102 Appendix A120 in the edition effective 31 January 2025, same wording): the term is used "where a seller is under compulsion to sell and that, as a consequence, a proper marketing period is not possible and buyers may not be able to undertake adequate due diligence. The price that could be obtained in these circumstances will depend upon the nature of the pressure on the seller and the reasons why proper marketing cannot be undertaken ... Unless the nature of, and the reason for, the constraints on the seller are known, the price obtainable in a forced sale cannot be realistically estimated ... A 'forced' sale is a description of the situation under which the exchange takes place, not a distinct basis of value." — [Red Book Global Standards, effective 31 January 2025](https://rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Red-Book-Global-Standards-incorporating-IVS.pdf)
- [FT] IVS on market evidence: "Sales in an inactive or falling market are not automatically 'forced sales' simply because a seller might hope for a better price if conditions improved"; and "While confirmed 'forced sale' transactions would generally be excluded from consideration in a valuation where the basis of value is market value, it can be difficult to verify that an arm's-length transaction in a market was a forced sale." — [Red Book 2025 edition, IVS 102 Appendix A120.04–A120.05](https://rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Red-Book-Global-Standards-incorporating-IVS.pdf)
- [FT] RICS (VPS 2 section 11 in the 2025 edition; VPS 4 section 10 in the 2022 edition): "The term 'forced sale value' must not be used." Forced sales arise "where there is pressure on a particular vendor to sell at a specific time – for example, because of the need to raise money or to extinguish a liability by a given date." "Any relationship between the price achievable by a forced sale and the market value is coincidental; it is not a valuation that can be determined in advance". A special assumption "that simply refers to a time limit for disposal without stating the reasons for that limit would not be a reasonable assumption to make." — [Red Book 2025 edition](https://rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Red-Book-Global-Standards-incorporating-IVS.pdf); identical text in the [2022 edition](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/2021_11_25_rics_valuation_global_standards_effective_2022.pdf)
- [FT] RICS on depressed markets: it is "a common misconception that in a poor or falling market there are automatically few 'willing sellers'"; sales by "administrators, liquidators and receivers" are by vendors "normally under a duty to obtain the best price in the current circumstances ... These sales will normally comply with the definition of market value." A marketing constraint "should not be confused with a forced sale". — [Red Book 2025 edition, VPS 2 paras 11.6, 11.9–11.10](https://rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Red-Book-Global-Standards-incorporating-IVS.pdf)
- [ABS] Liquidation value is a separate IVS basis: "The amount that would be realised when an asset or group of assets are sold on a piecemeal basis." — [IVSC glossary](https://ivsc.org/standards-glossary/)
- [FT] US practice on distressed comparables: Appraisal Institute guidance holds that foreclosures "can provide important information for appraisers" with appropriate adjustments, though appraisers in practice tend to avoid them; adjustments must be explained. — [Conklin, Coulson & Diop, "Distressed Comps"](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2022/preliminary/paper/B4N3kHnG)

### Inferences
- The standards give no percentage for a forced-sale discount and say none can be given in general; any "forced sale value = X% of market value" rule of thumb used by lenders is outside the standards.
- Under these definitions a bank or receiver sale that was properly marketed counts as market evidence; only a sale under a deadline that prevented proper marketing does not. For a model trained on registered sales, the standards imply excluding confirmed forced sales from the value benchmark while admitting that they are hard to identify from the register alone.
- Dubai court auctions open at an official valuation made "according to the current market price" (section 8), so the auction reserve is anchored to a market-value opinion, not to a forced-sale figure.

### Gaps
- The standalone IVSC document (IVS effective 31 January 2025) could not be opened directly (404); its text was read as reproduced in the RICS Red Book. An IVS exposure draft effective 31 January 2028 exists according to a search result but was not opened. [UNVERIFIED]
- No UAE-specific valuation standard (Emirates Book Valuation Standards / Taqyeem) was reviewed in this run.

## 8. Dubai, the UAE and the Gulf: evidence on distressed and auction discounts

### Takeaway
There is no academic or official measurement of distressed-sale discounts for Dubai; the evidence is press and broker commentary, which points to asking-price reductions of about 10% in early 2009, individual "distress" deals of 10–25% below market in soft years, and court or Land Department auctions that have repeatedly cleared 5–60% above their reserve prices. By law a Dubai mortgage auction opens at an official market valuation and the price can be stepped down by at most 25% over failed rounds, so deep auction discounts arise only when rounds fail.

### Cited Findings

**Legal mechanics of forced sales in Dubai**
- [PRESS — law firm, 18 Sep 2024] Court-supervised sale of mortgaged property under Dubai Law No. 14 of 2008: 30-day notarised demand, then 15 days' notice through the court; an expert, usually the Dubai Land Department, values the property "according to the current market price"; bidders deposit at least 20% of that evaluation; at the first session the highest bid must be at least the evaluation plus the mortgagee's expenses; if unsold the price is reduced "by 5% each time until it reaches a maximum reduction of 25%", after which the property is sold to the highest bid "provided it is not less than 50%" of the initial evaluation; the winner pays the balance within 10 days; any shortfall is claimed from the borrower separately; sales are typically held online through the Emirates Auction portal. — [BSA Law](https://www.bsalaw.com/insight/the-process-of-selling-a-mortgaged-property-through-a-court-supervised-auction-in-dubai/)
- [PRESS — law firm, 22 May 2012] At Land Department auctions bidders lodged a cheque for 20% of the reserve price (forfeited if the balance is not paid), paid the balance within 7 to 10 days, and could ask to view only if the property was not tenanted; "There are currently no detailed documents involved in the process." The first foreclosure under the 2008 law was tested in January 2010; the law "only appears to allow judicial sale for conventional banks", while Islamic lenders that own the asset can repossess and sell on their own timing. — [Hadef & Partners](https://hadefpartners.com/news-insights/insights/inside-the-dubai-land-departments-property-auctions/)
- [PRESS] Winning bidders must pay in cash with no provision to wait for a bank loan; Emirates Auction held a sale every week and the Land Department one every 60 days (2019). — [Gulf News, 27 Sep 2019](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/cityscape-global-bargains-to-be-had-at-dubai-property-auctions-1.66721129)
- [PRESS] Auctioned properties come "minus any payment or other obligations that the previous owner may have with the bank", but "any on-property issues ... will need to be met by the new buyer". — [Gulf News, 3 Jul 2023](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/want-to-buy-a-villa-on-dubais-palm-jumeirah-at-a-discount-try-the-auction-1.1688357658936)

**Auction outcomes**
- [PRESS] First bulk auction of repossessed homes, reported 7 Dec 2011: 8 foreclosed properties sold (villas plus two Dubai Marina apartments) for Dh740,000 to Dh6 million, all above reserve; one villa listed at Dh3.5m sold for Dh5.6m. Earlier attempts had failed because "Initially reserve prices were just too high" (head of valuations, Cluttons). Prices were then about 50% below peak in some places; hundreds of properties were in foreclosure but only one had previously been sold under the 2008 law, more than a year after the court order. — [The National, 7 Dec 2011](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/dubai-s-first-bulk-auction-of-repossessed-properties-1.450820)
- [PRESS] A report of a 28 November auction of court-forfeited freehold villas (year not shown on the page; consistent with 2011): base Dh1.1m sold Dh1.306m; base Dh1.5m sold Dh1.82m; base Dh3.5m sold Dh5.4m (18.7%, 21.3% and 54.3% above base, my arithmetic). — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/uae/forfeited-freehold-properties-going-going-gone-1.946918)
- [PRESS] 1 May 2012: of five Springs villas with unpaid mortgages, four sold for Dh1.2m–Dh2.26m, "about 15-30 per cent above the asking prices", with more than 160 people attending; more than 80 repossessed properties were expected to be auctioned that year. The law firm attending the 2012 auctions observed bids "5% to 40% above the reserve price". — [The National, 1 May 2012](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/dubai-villas-sold-at-auction-as-buyers-return-1.395799); [Hadef & Partners](https://hadefpartners.com/news-insights/insights/inside-the-dubai-land-departments-property-auctions/)
- [PRESS] A Land Department auction of court-ordered inheritance sell-offs: 21 properties offered, 11 sold (all leasehold; none of 8 freehold) for Dh85.69 million, 11.7% over base price; "Most participants are investors, as opposed to end-users." (Page shows only a "last updated 15 Sep 2018" date.) — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/dubai-land-department-auctions-11-properties-for-over-dh85m-1.1174801)
- [PRESS] 2019: an Emirates Hills villa sold at auction for Dh17.74m after 170 bids, at a rate described as "considerably lower" than at other times; a broker attributes cheap auction results partly to the property itself ("It could be the price, or it could be the design, elevation or layout that works against it"). — [Gulf News, 27 Sep 2019](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/cityscape-global-bargains-to-be-had-at-dubai-property-auctions-1.66721129)
- [PRESS] 2023: a Palm Jumeirah villa was offered at auction with a base price of Dh18m against Dh20–25m for comparable properties outside auction (10–28% below, my arithmetic; a starting price, not a sale price); "the base prices when they come up for auction represent a sizeable discount to current market rates" (investment adviser). — [Gulf News, 3 Jul 2023](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/want-to-buy-a-villa-on-dubais-palm-jumeirah-at-a-discount-try-the-auction-1.1688357658936)

**Private distressed sales by period**
- [PRESS] 2008–09 crash: in January 2009 a reporter could "knock about 10 per cent off several advertised prices" in Dubai (two-bedroom apartments on Palm Jumeirah Dh2.55m to Dh2.3m; Dubai Marina Dh2.3m to Dh2.1m) and up to 15% in Abu Dhabi; one Jumeirah Beach Residence apartment had already been cut by more than Dh1m to Dh2.6m since the summer; Dubai prices fell an average of 8% in the fourth quarter of 2008; sellers were mainly speculators exiting before the next instalment, and minimum deposits had risen from 10% to 25%. — [The National, 14 Jan 2009](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/home-sellers-offering-major-discounts-1.560652)
- [PRESS] 2015–16 slowdown: brokers advertised "distress" townhouses at Dh300,000 off Dh3m; distressed listings were 0.6% of all listings on a major portal; several agency heads saw no real sign of distressed selling ("The market is clearly down, but there is no crash"). — [The National, 9 Jan 2016](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/property/property-agents-bait-dubai-homebuyers-with-distress-deals-1.177373)
- [PRESS] 2019: brokers cited a Business Bay floor at 25% below market price; distress deals are generally cash with a 7 to 14 day turnaround; "everyone is terming every deal as distressed, which is wrong", partly because buyers compare with advertised prices when the transacted price is "always lower"; "mostly distressed properties have some kind of challenge attached with them". — [Gulf News, 15 May 2019](https://gulfnews.com/business/property/buying-property-in-dubai-is-a-distress-deal-a-good-deal-1.63916830)
- [PRESS] 2020–21: "We did have some distressed assets coming out of the Covid-19 lockdown ... Now I would say we are back up into early 2020, 2019 prices" (villa broker); a rating-agency analyst put prices 40–50% below the 2014 peak. — [Reuters via Gulf Times, 15 Mar 2021](https://www.gulf-times.com/story/686706/Buyers-return-but-Dubai-realty-faces-a-long-road-t)
- [PRESS — broker, updated 11 Sep 2026] Current cycle: genuine below-market distressed opportunities are "relatively rare during strong market cycles" and discounts "often marginal rather than dramatic"; distress is driven by "individual seller circumstances rather than systemic bank repossessions"; foreclosure-driven supply is "a relatively small portion of overall market activity"; the typical case is an investor-led resale, especially from off-plan owners near handover; named risks are hidden repair costs, outstanding service charges, title complications and "overestimating the true discount". — [Engel & Völkers Dubai](https://www.engelvoelkers.com/ae/en/resources/distressed-properties-dubai)

### Inferences
- Because the opening price is an official market valuation and the first round cannot clear below it, a Dubai mortgage auction is not by design a discount channel; the discount a buyer can expect is bounded by the step-downs (5% a round, 25% maximum, then a 50% floor) and depends on rounds failing. Every outcome found in the press (2011, 2012 and the inheritance auction) cleared above reserve, by 5–60%. Auction bargains therefore depend on (a) the official valuation lagging a rising market, (b) thin bidding in a falling market, or (c) properties others will not bid on.
- The costs that drive auction discounts elsewhere are present in Dubai in milder or different form: cash within 10 days, a 20% deposit at risk, limited or no inspection of occupied units, and the buyer bearing physical defects; against that, the buyer takes title free of the previous owner's bank debt. Applying the international evidence, a headline discount of 10–15% at a Dubai auction would be roughly what Cape Town and US studies find is needed to compensate for such costs and risks.
- Dubai's distressed supply is mainly private investors under payment pressure rather than bank stock, which matches the literature's "motivated seller" category (central range 5–12%) more than its "foreclosure" category; the press figures for genuine cases (10–17% off market, occasionally 25%) are consistent with that, and with the UK quick-sale range for 1–2 week cash completions.
- Press reports compare with asking prices or agents' opinions of market value, never with a model or matched sales, and a Dubai broker's own caveat is that the transacted price is "always lower" than the advertised one. The true discount in each quoted case is therefore smaller than stated by the normal negotiation margin (about 8–10% in the January 2009 examples).

### Gaps
- No academic study, regulator publication or research-house series (Knight Frank, CBRE, JLL, ValuStrat, Property Monitor) quantifying distressed or auction discounts in Dubai, the UAE or any Gulf market was found; searches returned index methodologies and market reports only.
- Two frequently cited Zawya articles could not be opened (HTTP 400) and are known only from search snippets: (i) 17 Apr 2011, saying that in 2009 and early 2010 speculators were ready to offload units "at even a 50 per cent discount" and that distress selling had ended; (ii) a 2020 piece saying owners were listing 10–15% below market during the pandemic while buyers offered 30–40% below asking. [UNVERIFIED]
- No aggregate statistics were found on the number, value, sell-through or price-to-valuation ratio of Dubai Courts / Emirates Auction / Land Department property auctions for 2020–2026; a broker blog claiming 10–25% typical auction discounts with reserves at 80–90% of valuation was seen only in a snippet and conflicts with the law-firm description. [UNVERIFIED]
- Dates of two Gulf News auction reports are unclear (pages show a 2018 "last updated" stamp for what appear to be 2011–2013 events), and the 2011 accounts differ slightly (Dh5.4m vs Dh5.6m for the top villa; five vs eight properties).
- No evidence was found on service-charge arrears, tenancy status or developer (off-plan) defaults as drivers of Dubai discounts.
